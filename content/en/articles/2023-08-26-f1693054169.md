In my memory, the faculty dormitory of National Chung Hsing University was my playground through the long summers of my childhood. Turning in from Zhongming South Road at the Nanmen Bridge, the main road stopped just before Ming-Te Home Economics and Commerce School, and from there extended a small looping lane. At the entrance to the residential area there seemed to be a signboard in black Kai-style characters reading "Xingda Fifth Village". Whether you walked clockwise or counterclockwise, you could circle all the scholars' dwellings. My grandparents' home in Taichung was the second one clockwise, with a red front door between grey concrete pillars.

Past the door was a corridor. On both sides, in the earth, my grandmother grew green rain lilies that often burst into white blossoms after the rain, clump after clump standing in rows, leading up to the door of the plain two-storey faculty home. Looking out from the front door, on the right stood a magnolia tree as tall as the building. Its branches were always scattered with magnolia buds. The low ones were often picked early in the morning to be offered before the family Buddhist altar, while the high ones bloomed freely at their fullest and most unrestrained moment, then fell as white petals into the corridor.

This corridor had many uses. In the evenings it was the playground where I, the little grandson, rode my tricycle, little feet pushing the front pedals round and round for many turns before completing one circuit. It was also the open-air dining table where my grandmother patiently fed me. I would take one mouthful for each lap, but as a child who didn't like eating, I went very, very slowly, and a bowl of rice often took more than half an hour, with my mouth still full of food.

In my memory of summer, this corridor was laid with long melons drying in the sun that Taichung never lacks. It was the season when cucumbers were plentiful. My grandmother and I would peel lots of the big yellow cucumbers she had bought, cut them in half, and in the fresh cucumber scent lay them out in rows on bamboo sieves, leaving them under the fierce summer sun of central Taiwan. Day after day, the cucumbers slowly wrinkled and shrank, their colour turning from fresh green to yellow, and finally to a shade just short of earthy brown, their shape flattened into ovals. Then they were packed into glass jars to pickle, with salt and sugar added and the lid screwed tight. A few months later they would become the pickled melon side dish seen at every meal, three times a day. Plain white rice without that melon was too bland for me.

In my memory of winter, this corridor became the pressing area for white rice batter. Glutinous rice soaked through with water was taken to the market to be ground into a white paste, then carried back in a bucket, packed in white cotton bags. My grandmother would press it with a long wooden bench and big stones, and slowly the water would seep through the outside of the cotton. Meanwhile she would be beside it, cutting winter white radish into fine shreds and stir-frying a little with fried shallots, and then she would mix in the thick rice batter, which I never knew how long it had been pressed. She then found every container in the house to fill: round ones, square ones, long strips. She lined them with gauze, poured in the batter one by one, and put them in the big steamer over high heat. As for me, apart from watching grandma make radish cake, I had long since lost patience and was playing alone to one side. In my memory, grandmother and I rarely spoke, yet we kept each other company. I knew grandma was busy, and grandma knew I was playing quietly.

The photo of the Chung Hsing University dormitory was taken in the 1970s. My memories are blurry and fragmentary, but I still remember that however much I did not want to eat, my grandmother fed me patiently. She never shouted at me, let alone said anything harsh. She was a traditional Taiwanese woman who lived through the Taisho, Showa and Republic eras. In those years of war and turmoil she never had schooling, and the women of that time did not say much. But through her long companionship I became familiar with her example of calmly observing the world and speaking little but acting in earnest. My character was shaped most deeply by my grandmother. From infancy to kindergarten, my early years and childhood were spent in her care.

If my life could be like my grandmother's, resilient, optimistic, warm, simple and healthy through a hundred years, that would be a lasting influence and blessing I owe to her for my whole life.

Thank you, Grandma. I love you. Let's be grandmother and grandchild again in the next life.
