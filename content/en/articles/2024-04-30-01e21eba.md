---
title: "Boss Lo's Travel Agency No. 005"
excerpt: "My first formal job in 1992 as a courier at an old Taipei travel agency, riding a scooter delivering visa documents, and what that time cost me in hindsight."
published: true
---

<p>My alma mater, Fu Jen Catholic University, is at No. 510, Zhongzheng Road, Xinzhuang City, Taipei County. That is part of Provincial Highway 1, the north-south trunk road, so by street number the school sits on the east side of Highway 1, and so on by extension.</p><p>In 1992, the summer between my first and second year of university, I took my first formal job with labour insurance: Daya Travel Agency. From the point of view of today's vocational students, it would count as a summer internship worth 3 credits, but back then I was only doing a part-time job to earn money, and the work did not stop when summer ended. I did not take my sociology coursework at Fu Jen very seriously, and since the job was mainly riding around delivering documents, I could always make a bit of extra money on the side.</p><p>Daya Travel Agency was an old travel agency in one of the many 12-storey apartment-style buildings around the junction of Minsheng East Road and Zhongshan North Road. Its registration number was 005. Walk in the door and there was a sofa reception area, and at a glance four or five desks. Apart from Boss Lo, who had a small partitioned office, and the accountant, who had a room of her own, the other colleagues sat at desks facing each other, working on and coordinating departures to various destinations. Smoking was allowed in the office, and Boss Lo and several colleagues smoked, so my impression is of an office always full of smoke and a bad smell, with a few dim, dust-caked fluorescent lights overhead. The whole place felt yellowed and out of date, like an old photographic negative.</p><p>I was the new errand boy, and the only university student in the company. My main work was nothing more than collecting and delivering documents. First thing in the morning I would find out where to collect from, mainly the documents customers needed for their visas. Before noon I would bring all the customer documents and fees back to the office and hand them to the OP (operations) lady, and then I could rest and get ready to take the sorted documents to the various visa offices in the afternoon. The afternoon routine was fairly fixed. The biggest batch of visa documents went first to the Interchange Association, Japan, on Jinan Road, perhaps with a few more customer items collected on the way. Then I would ride to Thai Airways on Dunhua North Road to handle Thai visas, and to the Malaysian visa office, whose location I can't quite remember now. After that the documents in my shoulder bag would be much fewer. Then I would go back to the office to chat and plan the next day's route. The work was easy, and I was on the road most of the time. In an age without mobile phones, it was a carefree job.</p><p>Anyone who has done courier work in the Taipei metropolitan area will know the rule of thumb "southeast even, northwest odd". Street numbers around the world are laid out with odd and even numbers on opposite sides of the street, so buildings on the same side all use odd or all use even numbers in sequence. That let us errand boys judge roughly how far a destination was from the size of the number, without having to strain over house numbers while also watching out for Taipei's terrifying traffic.</p><p>Today many of Taipei's main roads have bus lanes and MRT lines, and the traffic and cityscape are much tidier overall. What I disliked most about delivering by scooter in those days was Nanjing East Road, sections 1 to 5. From Yi-Die Department Store, Yong-Chi Department Store and Sincere Department Store through to Huan-Ya Department Store, almost all our partner companies were along Nanjing East Road, and the buses, trucks, cars and scooters stopping at the roadside fought lawlessly for lanes and parking spots. My poor, gallant little White 125 scooter accompanied me through nearly two years of courier life, through the streets and alleys of Taipei City and County, and later it often puffed white smoke and burned oil.</p><p>Thinking back now, I used my own scooter for company errands and rushed all over Taipei for only a little over ten thousand dollars a month, with the danger and exhaustion of being in the traffic besides. Thirty years ago university was hard to get into, yet after getting in I did not spend time confirming my career path, earning certificates, studying a specialty, or preparing for civil service or graduate school exams. Instead I squandered my young brainpower and energy on a meagre wage in the moment, which was not worth it at all. From the perspective of social mobility in sociology, this was really class fixity: I thought I was secure and ordinary at the time, but I was actually in the midst of risk without knowing it. Because of this ignorance, several amusing things happened. Reflecting on them now, if the me of that time were put into today's world, I might really have ended up in the news.</p><p>To be continued…</p>
