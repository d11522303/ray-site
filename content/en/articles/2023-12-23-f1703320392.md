---
title: "The Circular Sewing Machine in Our Living Room"
excerpt: "A childhood memory of my mother doing piecework on a circular sewing machine in our living room, and the small coins she left for us on its platform."
published: false
---

<p>My mum is only 19 years older than me. Her family was poor and large; my youngest aunt was born in the Year of the Pig, almost the same year as me, and there were so many aunts that we told them apart by the numbers two, three, four, five and six rather than by name. A big family was raised in the old house at Qiding on the Miaoli coast, and I only found out later that even the family surname handed down in the household had come through adoption. In such a crowded family of Qiding fishermen and itinerant cooks, almost all my uncles and aunts had little chance of schooling. After graduating from Zhaonan Junior High School in Miaoli, my mother went north to work in Guandu. Not yet an adult, she was one of the many women workers of that era of export-oriented economic growth.</p><p>In the 65th year of the Republic (1976), my parents, both working as operators at the Formosa Plastics Guandu textile plant, finally bought their first pre-sale flat on the fourth floor. That was our first home, and also the place where my mother left Formosa Plastics and turned to home piecework on the circular machine. I don't know when it began, but our home was full of pieces of fabric. Year after year the cloth changed with the seasons: in winter the house was piled with thin, light cloth of all kinds, and when the weather was hot, it was full of the knitwear panels instead. Bundle after bundle in every colour filled our fourth-floor home right out onto the balcony, and the house always smelled of wool and cloth. We children were used to a home that was like a warehouse, because the square stacks of cloth, taller than a person and reaching to the ceiling, were also our playground after school.</p><p>And my mother, only in her twenties, sat in front of the circular machine all the time, sewing endless piecework clothes.</p><p>To us children, the circular machine was an incongruous but necessary presence in the living room. It was a machine with sewing needles arranged all around its outer ring, with an electric motor driving the sewing unit and the rotating disc. Depending on how the garment was composed, my mother had to use the toothed clip in her hand to push the edges of two pieces of cloth clockwise into the teeth of the disc. It was repetitive work: one bundle of fabric joined to another bundle became part of a garment. When finished, the half-finished pieces fell to the floor, and we had to stack the finished semi-products, bundle them, and tie them tightly with plastic string into more bundles. Before long a trader would drive up to the street below, and we would throw the bundles of cloth from the fourth floor into the truck parked at the front on the ground floor, and the trader would collect them and take them away, probably to the next home workshop or factory.</p><p>How much could this earn? From memory, it was NT$1-2 for sewing a piece, depending on difficulty. So as the finished bundles flew from the balcony down onto the truck, I always imagined that it was NT$10, NT$20, NT$30 flying out. Thanks to my mother's sewing day and night and the time she gave, our family's income rose by more than a thousand dollars. Mum was very generous and trusted us children. In the middle of the circular machine there was a flat round platform (as in the screenshot, a picture I found online of a second-hand machine for sale). My mother would always leave some coins on the platform: big-head five-dollar coins, nickel one-dollar coins and brass fifty-cent coins. When the call of the old man selling Shandong steamed buns, or the bread van selling strawberry and spring onion bread, came up from the street below, we children could take the change and go and buy something to fill our stomachs. We were so content and happy.</p><p>Also, there is a short scar between the rim of my eye socket and my eyebrow, left when, as a naughty child chasing around at home, I crashed into the circular machine. I still remember that day I was playing at home with the neighbours or my younger brothers and sisters. There were pieces of cloth on the floor, my foot slipped, and my head went toward the machine. Luckily the needles on the disc were covered by a special iron frame, so I only hit the frame and bled. There were no stitches and no doctor; as time went by it became a scar and a mark left on my eye socket.</p><p>I don't remember how long this kind of home piecework lasted. I only remember the fourth-floor flat beside the high wall of the Far Eastern textile factory in Banqiao, piled with piecework cloth that changed with the seasons, and the alternation of colours and smells.</p>
