Bangkok Travel Day 4-1: Bangkok Central Station and Hong Sieng Kong Cafe

Putting lesser-known stops in an itinerary is my personal preference. Early in the morning we switched to the MRT subway to Hua Lamphong station, Bangkok's central station that was nearly demolished by the authorities. We then explored on foot a hip cafe and a creative-culture district near Chinatown, treating it as a half-day culture-and-history trip.

Bangkok Central Station is the starting point of Thai railways. Its construction was personally supervised by King Chulalongkorn, Rama V, and it was the origin of Thailand's railways. Before the pandemic, the Eastern and Oriental Express also used this station as the starting point of luxury journeys to Laos or to Malaysia and Singapore. The station building, completed in 1916, was designed by two Italian architects modelled on Frankfurt Central Station. It is a terminus station with 7 platforms and 14 tracks. Although it is now half-abandoned with few trains, entering the hall through the side door you can still feel its past prosperity. The main building's platform area is joined to a huge steel arched roof. A few ticket windows still sell tickets, but there are no entry gates, so you can come and go on the platforms freely, and to use the toilet you pay 2 baht to the attendant at the door. Beside the exit there is a small museum and a little shop selling railway souvenirs; those interested can take a look, and it is no loss if you miss it.

After leaving Hua Lamphong station, we strolled off to find a well-known hip cafe. Walking around near Chinatown, the scenery is much like Taipei's Chifeng Street area in Zhongshan District, with traditional machinery and equipment shops and completely abandoned 19th-century buildings, and of course some that have been converted by people with vision into small creative-culture shops. After wandering through the whole district, we felt that the creative and design scene in Taipei's Zhongshan area is superior, and may be far less profound than the Dadaocheng area. Still, if, like us, you find that Bangkok's prices have risen to be more expensive than Taipei's and that besides massage there is hardly anything cheap to buy, then having flown three thousand li away, you can come to Thailand to experience an environment of Fujian and Teochew immigrants, and feel such similarity even after several hundred years.

The Hong Sieng Kong cafe can only be found on foot, and I did not see any tuk-tuks entering the alleys along the way, so I put the location in the comments. It is said to have been a hundred-year-old house that ran a charcoal business. You first see the Qingshui Zushi (Chinese Buddhist) temple and then notice a small blue shop beside it. But the door is only small, and once inside it is very different, and someone will block the counter and not let you go in to look until you have ordered and paid. After the family each ordered a dish, we found a seat in the only air-conditioned little house to eat. For the other lovely views, please look at the photos. The food is not bad, and the overall atmosphere and the way the place is run are first-class, even the toilet is air-conditioned. I recommend it to anyone visiting Bangkok.
