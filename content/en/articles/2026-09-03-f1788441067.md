3/697 The Zeigarnik Effect

Do you, like me, often have a very real feeling when preparing teaching materials or working: when something is interrupted halfway, it keeps nagging at the back of your mind, yet something you have just finished handling you can't recall any details of the moment you turn around? This is the 【Zeigarnik Effect】.

The Zeigarnik Effect was proposed by the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik (1900-1988) while observing the behaviour of restaurant waiters. She found that waiters remembered orders very clearly before the customer paid, but forgot them completely once the bill was settled. In teaching or game design, this kind of unfinished suspense (a cliffhanger) is actually very useful. Many games deliberately leave small tasks at the end of a level, or show a progress bar that has reached only 67%, to keep people from letting go.

It is the same in learning. If you pause slightly in teaching at the moment when things are going most smoothly or when curiosity is highest, the brain keeps digesting the unfinished information while it rests. Besides the nagging feeling of not having finished, there is also a natural urge to complete the task, which in academia is called the Ovsiankina Effect. For example, when students habitually procrastinate on the opening materials or a large pile of assignments, the solution is very simple: set a micro-goal, such as reading just two lines or writing just a title. Once the task has started, the momentum afterwards often carries on by itself.

In fact, I often push my own progress forward with countdowns or series, which is exactly this effect, playing tricks on myself. From a practical learning perspective, the sense of incompleteness is useful but cannot be abused. The latest research also notes that this effect mainly provides the drive for people to start again (Task Resumption), but it may also leave people in constant anxiety.

If you want to understand more of the psychological thinking behind Zeigarnik, you can refer to this 2020 paper. Knowledge is really fascinating, and especially so because the source, an academic journal, is far more credible than random hearsay and random writing.

MacLeod, C.M. Zeigarnik and von Restorff: The memory effects and the stories behind them. Mem Cogn 48, 1073–1088 (2020). https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-020-01033-5
