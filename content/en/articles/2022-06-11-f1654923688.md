The Platform to Protect Taiwan's Education is a platform that Professor Tai Po-Fen of the Department of Sociology at Fu Jen Catholic University works hard on. As an alumnus of Fu Jen's sociology department who is also an employee of a private vocational school, I find myself in a conflicting role.

For someone who works at a school, of course the financial security of a private school matters most (so this chart is ranked by the rightmost column, from the green, blue and cyan zones through yellow to the red-light zone). Personally, I also feel that for students, what matters most is having teachers who can teach with peace of mind.

In addition, the student-teacher ratio and enrolment rate that the Ministry of Education often publishes can be adjusted and controlled, so they are only for reference. Whether students will still have an alma mater in the future depends, for a private school, on whether it has the money to keep operating.

:::Student-teacher ratio:::

Just look at the colours and you can see that a school's student-teacher ratio and its financial security are inversely related. In short, the more students per class on average, the more money the school naturally makes and the healthier its finances. So judging teaching quality by the student-teacher ratio is not necessarily accurate. The theory that teaching quality requires teachers not to teach too many students probably only applies in the environment of public universities.

:::Enrolment rate:::

The enrolment rate is even more open to debate. A private school that has been reinvested in by a business group may enrol only a hundred or so students across the whole school, with all its teachers on project-based contracts, and of course the numbers look good.

:::Financial security:::

The Ministry of Education is very conservative about this information and rarely mentions it. The platform openly hopes the figures will be announced as soon as possible. We who draw private-school salaries only know to work hard at recruiting and retaining students, keep teaching quality up, and provide internships and lifelong service to graduates. Only then does the school's finances have a moment of security, and it is only a moment.
