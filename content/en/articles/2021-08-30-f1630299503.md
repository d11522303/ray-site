---
title: "In Defence of the University Entrance Exam"
excerpt: "After an online course on the 108 Curriculum, Ray argues that exam-based selection will always exist, and that constantly changing the system is the real problem."
published: true
---

I am taking my school's online course, which is about how to teach under the 108 Curriculum Guidelines in technical senior high schools. The lecturer mentioned that only the two sides of the Taiwan Strait still have a joint university entrance exam, so the curriculum's learning portfolio is meant to let students accumulate material from their everyday studies, giving most students a route into higher education through application and screening. But I am very doubtful about this claim.

In fact, in any country that needs intellectual, elite talent, selecting by exam scores will always exist. The curriculum should be designed around the way humans like to compete and be ranked, rather than designing a pile of schemes in an attempt to replace examinations. Exams have their reasonable side, and how to select talent fairly is an important issue for advanced countries.

In South Korea, the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) is held at the end of December every year to decide which university a student gets into. Japan is even tougher: every January there is the Common Test for University Admissions, and after it each university runs and decides on its own individual exams to admit students, using so-called deviation scores to define the entry standard. In the UK, the A-Level exams are held in May, and this standard is used by graduates choosing which universities to enter (related reports are in the comments).

I am not trying to start a fight. I just feel there is nothing wrong with the joint entrance exam; what is wrong is changing the system again and again.
