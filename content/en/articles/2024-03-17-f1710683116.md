Have you heard of the Technological University Star Plan (the Fanxing admission channel)?

At dinner, three local high school students suddenly appeared at our table: two from vocational high schools and one from a general high school, all classmates of Little Doggy from elementary or junior high school. After greeting us very politely, they seemed to want to speak but hesitated. My wife kindly asked whether something was up, and the students then haltingly said they wanted to hear our advice and views on their further studies.

We chatted for nearly an hour over dinner, and from them I felt deeply how troubling the current exam and admissions system is for students. Whatever they decide right now will determine the direction of their professional studies for the next four years. They are weighing how each school looks to the outside world and to themselves, the expectations of society and family, and the job prospects and earning prospects after graduation.

One of the vocational high school students ranks in the top three of his school, so he will apply to universities through the technological Star Plan. The variety of admission channels troubles every vocational high school student. The complicated exams and channels have nearly made both teachers and students give up on preparing for each kind. When I asked whether their school teachers offered guidance, the answer was yes, but he doesn't quite understand it and the teacher helps arrange the procedures. Indeed, in recent years, with career guidance aimed at developing each student's aptitude, vocational high school teachers have taken on considerable responsibility for students' professional and career choices. But if teachers are unfamiliar with industry developments and trends and lack practical work experience, it is hard for them to give accurate advice, and they can only counsel from their own understanding and from training workshops.

So in our chat I found the students had heard things from teachers such as: you can make a lot of money doing opinion polls? Doing a two-day-class programme, five days on and two off, to get a diploma is also an option? And other plausible-sounding but wrong ideas.

Many scholars and people in education believe the current exam and admissions system is a design that cannot be avoided for students' development! If teachers only ask students to focus on academic scores, and emphasising GSAT, vocational exam or subject-test results means the high school teachers aren't trying hard enough, then I really hope the scholars who designed these systems would come to a senior high or vocational school and do a full year of fieldwork before going back to be professors.

Looking at the current system, however admissions change, for forty years the university entrance exam has still existed in the form of the revised GSAT and subject tests. If teachers and students prefer not to overthink, then staying the course has almost become the unshakeable truth. But if you have students who think more, paired with teachers who want to help, then these nearly crazy derivative system designs really do become a trouble.

If parents and students find the complicated system hard to master, and students can't get clear answers from their school teachers, I suggest looking at the system simply from three angles.

1. Academic ability is hard power. Study hard for the university GSAT and the vocational high school exam. Scores are like money: you never have too many. If you score more than others, you have more choices than they do.

2. For your learning portfolio, follow your interests. Only by finding your own traits and interests can you develop a good learning process. Don't believe the paid solutions on the market.

3. Be very practical in choosing a department. Whether the university connects with well-known internship companies, or has very thorough career guidance, are key factors. If you are fascinated by famous schools, please make sure your family lacks nothing at home except a diploma.
