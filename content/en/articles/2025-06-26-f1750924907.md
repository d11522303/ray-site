Thoughts on the Training Week Before Summer Break

The students are already on summer vacation. Today the department head talked with me about Teacher Hsiao-Hua wanting to resign. This is already the second time she has raised it. I keep thinking of the reluctance and helplessness I felt in the past when good colleagues left. Teachers during my teaching career, such as Lu Chiang-Chuan and Wu Chi-Wen at Taiwan Hospitality & Tourism College, and Hu Ching-Yu and Kang Po-Kai at Hsing Wu, have all given me a great deal to think about as I stay in my current post.

I have three points to think about:

First, have a chat with the person. After working together for so long there must be a lot of unspoken understanding. Many words and matters go unsaid because we always think we will meet again tomorrow or next week and there will be a chance to say them later. But once a colleague's resignation is confirmed, things left unsaid become awkward later. At this critical moment, be sure to have a meal together, and if you think of it, give a small gift. I hope that a colleague can become a lifelong friend, so that before the thread of friendship is let out longer, both sides have a chance to tie the end a little tighter.

Second, the mindset of putting yourself in the other's place and communicating. On a university campus, incidents like this happen every year, all the time. Some say campuses are simpler than society, but in fact each has its own strengths. Teachers at a school have high self-esteem and high self-expectations, so handling interpersonal matters requires even more care and consideration. When communication can no longer continue, leave others a way out in everything, and the road will naturally open. When I was department head, I seldom wanted to discuss matters like this with colleagues, always feeling I should just handle it myself. But I never once handled it well, because I am not good at management at all and find it hard to read what people need. At that time I thought I was good at communicating and that a chat or some method would settle things. What those three years actually taught me was that I had talk without communication, understood people poorly, and was bad at dealing with people.

The third thing I learned is team building. In these days of training there were several presentation sessions, and the speakers all mentioned teams. Public schools value professors' research and development, industry-academia cooperation and professionalism, so teams there are basically task-oriented. Private schools handle recruitment and teaching together. With the falling birth rate, recruiting is already hard, and almost every student is welcomed in person. The key to hoping they will not drop out, withdraw or transfer during their four years of study is team building. How do you build a team on campus? For teachers and students to stay by each other, and for students to have a sense of belonging, honour and identification with the campus and the teachers, we must start with the teacher team: communicate selflessly when things come up, find a suitable way out through disagreements, support each other in teaching and activities, and even exchange information about various subsidies and awards. None of these can be neglected. And when students achieve something or teachers win awards, credit the team. Everyone's importance should be counted and treasured, and having the chance to go to and leave work together is fate.

I know I am a private school teacher. It is not that we don't care about publishing and research ability, but when your job is not secure from month to month, some things have to be traded off. Starting next week I will welcome two months of paid summer vacation. I hope that when next year's Facebook memories come around, or when school starts in September, Teacher Hsiao-Hua, Teacher Fan, Director Huang and the rest of us will still be one team, steadily recruiting and teaching amid the great tide, and adding colour to ourselves through the growth and achievements of our students.
