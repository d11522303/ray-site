Nothing in Excess (Delphic Maxims)

My old employer, the China Credit Information Service (CCIS), recently published its latest market survey report!

Do you know which three companies had the highest restaurant revenues in Taiwan in 2020, under the pandemic? How has the pandemic changed the food-service landscape, and how will it develop in the future? The report is in the comments, if you're interested in reading it.

Every year, CCIS compiles the Top 5000 Corporations in Taiwan from various objective sources. In 1997 I was still an assistant researcher in CCIS's marketing department, responsible for writing the report each year, and also for reorganising this market-valuable data to help companies with market analysis. The companies I helped write listing proposals for included Sakura Construction, Chingyun Electronics and Zuanchuan Enterprise; those I helped with capital increases after listing included Taiwan Fengyuan (Tai Feng), Tuntex and Evergreen Enterprise (then the operator of the airport MRT). Many of these companies have since been delisted or disappeared. I still remember going to their headquarters to report or negotiate, and the looks those companies gave a market research firm. But isn't work really just about everyone earning a meal?

The most memorable project, as I recall, was when the Nomura Research Institute commissioned CCIS to write a report on the industrial impact of Taiwan joining the GATT (now the WTO). The budget was so small that CCIS could only assign a new recruit to write it, otherwise the cost would not have been covered. But the interviews were so interesting that I gave it my all to study every kind of news report and research. Much of the academic literature was so fragmentary as to be useless. For this report I shuttled between the office, the National Central Library and the World Trade Center library, and it took at least three months, day and night, to finish the Chinese version. Nomura Research Institute and the Interchange Association, Japan then translated it into Japanese and published it in Japan as an industry reference. I still have, on hand, the blood and sweat of that work, typed word by word.

When I worked at CCIS in 1997, Taipei still had no MRT system. I rented a place on Sanhe Road in Sanchong, and every day rode with tens of thousands of other scooter commuters across Taipei Bridge to work in Songshan District. I started at 9 a.m. and finished at 9 p.m., at least six days a week, the same as the "996" work schedule that has become a fashionable term in China today. It was truly a realistic experience of a young Taiwanese graduate "selling their liver" for work. With low pay and long hours, I don't remember how much money I made! And I left no memories of life from that time either!

The harshest reality is that in those two years at CCIS, I began losing my hair and slowly lost the full head of hair of my youth, which was hardly worth the trade.
