Vocational Education in the Wave of Generative AI

This issue of Nature discusses the future of universities. The article I care about most is about AI's effect on students when it comes to exams. Nature hopes universities will play a good role in society rather than a bad one, but with phones and AI, in the high-stakes testing of many university course midterms or finals that affect GPA, AI can create a lot of unfairness if teachers do not manage it properly, because cheating may become much easier. During this final exam week, rumours have been circulating on Dcard from several top universities of people caught using AI to solve problems in important exams and failed for it.

Nature suggests that long-term, ongoing assessment in teaching (Continuous Assessment) can be an effective alternative. For example, medical education has long used continuous assessment widely. During clinical rotations, medical students are continuously assessed by senior physicians, and mentors observe their clinical reasoning, teamwork and communication with patients. These observations, combined with written reflections and peer evaluation, can give a full picture of how a student's abilities change over time. However, this model is still rare in other disciplines, mainly because it greatly increases teachers' workload.

The tourism and hospitality disciplines are mostly in private vocational education. If teachers were to adopt long-term Continuous Assessment, the closest thing, I think, is what our department has been doing in recent years: the "progressive certification planning and internship-employment guidance system". But carrying it out is really hard work, and it cannot be achieved in one class or one academic year. It needs every teacher in the department to work together, with teachers' effort and students' self-discipline in every class every semester before it can be put into practice.

So most vocational institutions still need medium- and high-stakes testing (High-Stakes Testing) to assess the abilities of learners or future practitioners, for example by guiding students to take national licensing exams, such as the MOTC tour leader and tour guide exam and the Ministry of Labor certificates. This is a test model with professional-competence benchmarks, so in class I often tell vocational students that getting a tour leader licence is more meaningful than getting a university diploma.

If you are a student with a real "interest and aspiration" in travel and aviation, the Hsing Wu Department of Travel Management is currently open for the few places in its transfer-student exam. You are welcome to apply and experience what the "progressive certification planning and internship-employment guidance system" is. But be mentally prepared: you will have to stay after class for lessons, take licences you can never finish taking, prepare internship paperwork you can never finish preparing, and listen to endless reminders from teachers until you are fed up.
