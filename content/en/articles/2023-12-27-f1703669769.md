【The Back-Track Class at Yongzhong】

When I was in first year at the back-track class of a Yonghe junior high, fighting was part of daily life. Small and weak, I was afraid of fights. Every day I watched my classmates brawl, either among ourselves or in mass fights with the class next door, and because of my frail body I developed the ability to hide whenever I could and dodge whenever I could. I also had good friends in the class, and the best of them was Coach Yu. In junior high he was well built and on the school team, and he also attended the remedial class in the homeroom teachers' office after school, so in that frightening learning environment I always tried to stay with him: going to class, going to the toilet, chatting between classes, and going to play table tennis together. In truth my physical fitness was terrible; I could not run, could not run fast, and had no strength in my hands. In a boys' school, without a decent circle of friends I would probably have been the skinny boy who got his trousers pulled down by bullies.

These past few days a shocking murder involving a junior high student happened in New Taipei, and when I saw the news I was suddenly pulled back to being that skinny, unconfident and frightened 12-to-13-year-old. Junior high teachers 40 years ago all came to class with canes, and to this day I still cannot forgive the teachers for the corporal punishment that I, who never grew tall in those years, suffered. But this time, in the fear of campus incidents, I suddenly came to agree that perhaps a teacher carrying something on them was not only about authority but also self-defence. Perhaps campuses back then really were very unsafe.

Coach Yu was the source of my sense of security throughout junior high. The entry classes then were sorted by rank. The whole year seemed to have thirty-some classes. Among classes 1 to 16, the front-track classes, the even numbers were A+ classes and the odd numbers were A- classes, and from class 17 onwards they were all B-track classes. The two of us were placed in class 26. In fact the quality of the students in the class was not nearly as bad as the classification implied, but once a class number becomes a label on campus, differential treatment naturally follows, and students' later development could be ranked in this small society because of how adults had sorted them.

In first year I was terrified to be placed in this class, and also surprised, because I really did not know what I had done wrong to be sorted like this. My classmate Coach Yu was in the same class as me in first year and stayed at school to study with me. When classmates fought I instinctively looked for him, staying as far from the storm in the class as possible and taking care not to be dragged in. Of course there were times when I was bullied until I went mad and fought back. Our class was nearest the school gate, and classmates often leaned on the parapet watching the discipline chief by the gate beating students with a cane. At the same time, some annoying and rowdy classmates would deliberately grab the railing, wrap up the small and weak me, and thrust hard against me from behind. Once I got so angry that I turned around and we wrestled; in the end it was Coach Yu who came and pulled us apart, stopping the bullying between me and that classmate that I had no way of answering.

Of course, later I also stopped studying and loved to play. I remember a few times sneaking out of school to play snooker, and I don't remember whether he came along, until I was apparently discovered by the homeroom teacher and made to stay after school, and that was when things turned around.

Our homeroom teacher was a trainee fresh from Normal University in her first year of assignment, and she was full of passion and enthusiasm for teaching us. From some point on, several of us were asked to go to the office after class to study and do homework, and from then I slowly returned to the proper track of learning, finding my way back to study from a lost and frightening campus. Textbook by textbook I slowly read through and understood them, and began moving up, level by level. In second year I first moved to class 13, an A- class, leaving the B-track group; after placing in the top 2 in the year's grades in the A- class, in third year I could move to an A+ class. In three years of junior high I changed classes three times: three different world views, three completely different sets of classmate characteristics.

Only as an adult did I learn that it wasn't that I had done something wrong; we simply had no right to choose to enter an A+ class.

About ten years ago, Coach Yu encouraged me to go back to Yonghe Junior High on Teachers' Day to find my first-year homeroom teacher, and only then did I dare walk into the campus I remembered only for the sound of canes and the atmosphere of fights. In fact schools at every level today are more peaceful and happier than before; this is the improvement of the times and of parent-child education, and even more the progress of a civil society. It is a pity that I again saw saddening incidents on campus in New Taipei in the news. I thought of the unease of 40 years ago, and I worry even more about the future development of the education scene.

Coach Yu and I both remain on campuses now, doing our best to hold on with our small strength. I think he, like me, does not want students to be sorted into the wrong environment for no reason and nearly lose the chance to find themselves again.
