2025.7.28

Bangkok Travel, Day 1

Travelling at the turn of the semester seems to be the norm, a search for a little rest.

We chose Bangkok, Thailand because nobody in the family had been there, and Taiwanese media reports of earthquakes and crime had made Bangkok flights very cheap. Round-trip tickets on Starlux Airlines cost only about NT$8,000 per person, and with reasonable accommodation costs, we decided to visit the City of Angels, which ranks first in the world for international visitor numbers. For our seven nights we chose the Maitria Hotel, at the intersection of Asok and Sukhumvit. The hotel website says Maitria is Sanskrit for loving-kindness and friendship. The booking on Booking.com came with an airport pickup. After leaving the terminal, most pickup companies list your name on an A4 sheet of paper, and when you arrive they tell you to wait another 5-10 minutes for the driver and licence plate number. There was no need to transfer to the Airport Rail Link or push three pieces of luggage, and we went by car straight to the door of the Maitria Hotel. Both the pickup driver and the front desk were very friendly all the way, introducing their hometown Bangkok to our family, and today happened to be the Thai king's birthday, a national holiday, and a day without rain in the rainy season, so it was very comfortable and pleasant.

We smoothly exchanged Thai baht and bought BTS rabbit cards at the airport B1 and 2F. Tomorrow we will go and see this huge tourist city. For dinner we strolled to the nearby Terminal 21 and ate at Kub Kao Kub Pla Thai Cuisine (meaning local side dishes to go with rice). I forgot to ask for less spicy or no spice, and was startled by ordinary Thai spiciness. The food at this restaurant is delicious, and four dishes for 1,000 baht was just right. I recommend it to those who dare to eat spicy food. Later we went to the Terminal 21 food court for more, and the cheap eats were equally tasty.

During the meal, my family asked what Thailand's economy mainly relies on. I looked it up and learned that Thailand is dominated by services, with tourism, finance and transport accounting for nearly 60% of GDP, followed by over 30% from industrial exports such as automobiles and parts. I thought agricultural exports, which are important, were under 10%. But this is the surface of the economy. Thai people not in the export business may care more about daily subsistence. The resilience of an ancient civilisation comes from the accumulation of a thousand years of culture and self-respect, and I really envy Thais' identification with their own ethnic group and country. In the next few days I'll have the chance to walk around the city more to understand it, resting and learning at the same time.
