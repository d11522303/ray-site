[In Support of a Moderate Opening of the Teacher-Training Exemption]

The dignity and security of the teaching profession have long been eroded by legislation. I suggest we simply go all the way and abolish the Teachers' Act too. Teaching doesn't need a special law to regulate it. Just let the Employment Services Act govern teachers: pay for what is done, and dismiss with severance, according to law, those who do not do well. It would be even better if public and private school teachers all became workers, and I would raise both hands in favour of merging labour insurance and public-servant insurance years of service, so that working years can accumulate and everyone's future protection is more uniform. That would spare us the occupational antagonism and the unfair pension arrangements in society that cannot be improved because we are stuck fast under unsuitable regulations.

I was originally a daytime office worker. By a twist of fate, a classmate brought me into a five-year junior college to teach weekly as a part-time instructor in the evening division, and now and then I went to different schools as an industry instructor to share experience in the tourism and leisure industry. For a class period or two, just chatting about the joys and troubles of that day's work and my work experience was enough to hold the students' attention at the time, and I thought I was well suited to be a teacher.

When I actually became a full-time teacher at a school, in the first semester I still taught in a similar way, but in less than a month I ran out of material, and I could feel that the students were not interested. So I asked many senior colleagues how to teach and how to arrange a course. They gave me many positive suggestions and plans, and I realised that industry stories and management experience can only be treated as garnish for teaching. Exchanging work experience and interaction alone eventually leaves things too stale, with no structured teaching progress and no clear roles between students and teacher. So standing at the lectern means much more than just lecturing: there is classroom management, course planning, staged progress, assignments and assessment of learning outcomes, and finally, after an 18-week course ends, you have to accept students' evaluations to see whether they were satisfied with your class.

A short explanation cannot make clear the ability and professionalism of a teacher who faces students over the long term. But I too became a university teacher without going through teacher training (that is also the system: university teachers do not need to go through teacher training). Even so, I support the Ministry of Education's policy of letting industry instructors enter campuses without teacher training, because the press has reported that many teachers hired through the teacher-training system have proved unsuitable. So I recommend that anyone with a heart for education work, whether capable and experienced professionals or managers, come into the campus and try teaching, and see this low-paid, multitasking work environment that nevertheless brings a bit of a sense of achievement, with students who are absurd and adorable.

By Ruey-Feng Chen
