The focus of the first class was the "recognition" of objects and faces. Recognition has nothing to do with whether you can see; it is the result of specific neurons in the brain carefully analysing the shape, appearance and angle of the information passed along the optic nerve. So patients with face blindness and object blindness actually have normal eye function; the defect lies in the brain regions responsible for processing and analysing objects or faces.

Also, we often joke about ourselves being "face blind", but in fact face blindness refers only to the symptoms caused by damage to the specific brain regions described in class. Our condition is at most a weaker "ability to recognise face shapes", which falls within the range of normal individual differences. The main reason relates to neural plasticity: face recognition relies mainly on a brain structure called the fusiform gyrus, and in people who are good at recognising faces this area is more active, and so has stronger neural connections. As for situations such as Chinese people being better at recognising Chinese faces than telling apart Europeans and Americans, this too relates to the weaker connections in this part of the brain to ethnic groups we are unfamiliar with.

The second class introduced red-green colour blindness (and colour weakness). The teacher said this is an X-linked genetic disease, so the incidence in men is much higher than in women. Thinking about it, I realised that all the classmates and friends I have met with red-green colour blindness (or weakness) were men, and I had never thought it was due to this reason! Because the teacher mainly introduced colour blindness in class, afterwards I looked up supplementary material on colour weakness. The main difference between colour weakness and colour blindness is abnormal cone cell function (insufficient light sensitivity), classified in detail as follows: the most common red-green colour weakness can be divided into protanomaly and deuteranomaly. Protanomaly is abnormal function of the L-type (long-wavelength) cone cells responsible for sensing red light, so red appears dim and is easily confused with green; deuteranomaly is abnormality of the M-type (medium-wavelength) cone cells responsible for sensing green light, so the red and green areas are easily confused. The rarer blue-yellow colour weakness is abnormality of the S-type (short-wavelength) cone cells responsible for sensing blue light, so blue is easily confused with green, yellow and pink.

The last piece of knowledge I found really cool: the reason we think the moon near the ground looks bigger is that our brain assumes "the sky near the horizon is farther away than the sky overhead". Although this perception goes against intuition, the orbit illusion introduced in class verifies that it really exists and is correct in our perception. As for why the brain assumes the sky overhead is closer, from the extra material I looked up: when perceiving the sky, the brain imagines it as a hemispherical dome like an upside-down bowl, so we feel the sky is a dome at a limited distance that we could touch; and in our visual perspective experience, things nearer the horizon are farther away, which produces the illusion that the sky near the horizon is farther than the sky overhead. In addition, I learned that because there are more reference objects near the horizon (such as buildings, trees, mountains and so on), these make the brain judge the moon to be far behind them, which is also an important factor in the moon size illusion.
