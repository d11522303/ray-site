---
title: "Why Naming an Insurance Beneficiary Matters"
excerpt: "Always designate a beneficiary rather than writing only 'statutory heirs': it reflects who you love now, keeps the payout out of your estate, and prompts regular reviews."
published: true
---

<p><b>Key idea: always designate a beneficiary; don't just write "statutory heirs"</b></p><p>When you buy insurance for yourself as a young person on a modest budget, besides the <b>policyholder</b> and the <b>insured</b>, there is a field for the <b>beneficiary</b>. The person in this role will often change over your life. When you are young it may be your parents or the relatives who raised you; after marriage it may be your spouse; once you have children, your children are your dearest; and in old age you may not want to leave any money to the next generation at all, perhaps wanting it to go to the caregiver who has looked after you all along.</p><p>First, let's talk about what statutory heirs are. At the moment the insurance payout is needed, you are no longer in this world, so if the beneficiary is written only as statutory heirs, the payout simply becomes part of the inheritance. According to Article 1138 of the Civil Code, heirs to an estate, apart from the <b>spouse</b>, are determined in this order: 1. Direct descendants (children / adopted children / grandchildren). 2. Parents. 3. Siblings. 4. Grandparents.</p><p>Under the above distribution rules of the Civil Code, the spouse has the greatest rights. If there are children, they can share the estate equally with the spouse; otherwise everyone else must wait until the spouse has received 50% of the estate before dividing the remainder. Note also that this places great weight on the status of legal spouse: ex-partners and divorced persons do not count, and with the arrival of diverse family forms, roles differ from the traditional view. So if you are someone's spouse, make sure you legally hold that status; registration with the household registration office is crucial.</p><p><b>So why designate a beneficiary?</b></p><p><b>1. Because the one you love most keeps changing</b></p><p>After you pass away, whatever people or things you care about most can become your beneficiary, so besides natural persons you can also name an organisation or a legal entity. Nowadays, for many policies where the policyholder and the insured are both yourself, you can easily change the beneficiary online or in a mobile app.</p><p><b>2. A designated beneficiary receives the death benefit, which is not counted as part of the estate</b></p><p>If your dearest is your spouse and you do not designate a beneficiary, then after you are gone this insurance money will become part of the estate and may be subject to 10-20% estate tax. (For details see the Ministry of Finance's <a target="_blank" rel="noopener" href="https://www.etax.nat.gov.tw/etwmain/tax-info/understanding/tax-saving-manual/national/estate-and-gift-tax/QbA7Lqp">introduction to estate tax</a>.)</p><p><b>3. You can come back from time to time to rethink your insurance planning</b></p><p>Often we buy insurance without much thought: we are introduced to a policy and buy it on the spot, and after several or a dozen years we have no idea why we keep paying premiums. So you can set a mid-year or year-end review of your finances: log in to the website, look at the contents of your policies and your beneficiary arrangements, and think about your relationship with your family. This helps you appreciate the value of being a human being.</p>
