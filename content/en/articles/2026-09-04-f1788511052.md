4/696 Lev S. Vygotsky

Lev Vygotsky (1896-1934) was a Soviet psychologist.

His most important theory is the famous Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) model of cognitive development. He divided learners into two levels:

Actual development: the level at which a learner can solve problems independently, without anyone's help.

Potential development: the maximum limit that can be reached with the help of an experienced adult or peer (MKO, More Knowledgeable Other).

In 1976, Jerome Bruner and others, building on Vygotsky's ZPD model, went further and proposed the term scaffolding: teachers should provide appropriate support (hints, examples, guidance) within the learner's ZPD, and gradually withdraw support as the learner's independence develops, so that learners can reach the limit of their potential development through the guidance of an MKO.

But Vygotsky contracted tuberculosis, then an incurable disease, in his twenties. Racing against time through extreme physical discomfort, he researched and published his findings and views. In 1934 his condition worsened severely, and almost bedridden, he dictated the final chapters of his major work, Thought and Language, which his wife and assistants recorded. He died at only 37.
