---
title: "Boss Lo and His Friends: The Travel Agency's Delivery Boy"
excerpt: "My early-1990s university job as a travel agency document courier in Taipei, delivering visa and passport papers by scooter, and what it taught me."
published: true
---

<p>Last summer I bought a new Yamaha JOG scooter and went on Foodpanda to register an account, take the online test and sign up, intending to do some part-time delivery over the summer. After all, I started out as a delivery man, only what I delivered wasn't food but all kinds of visa documents for a travel agency.</p><p>Thirty-some years ago, anyone who wanted to travel abroad had to arrange a lot of paperwork. Besides a passport, whether you were going to nearby places like the Philippines, Malaysia, Thailand, Japan or Korea, or farther afield to the US, Canada or Europe, every country required a visa, so for a trip through several European countries the visa fees alone were a considerable expense. But that was exactly what kept us errand boys in business.</p><p>In 1992-1993 (Republic of China years 81-82) I was studying in the day division of a university. The content of my department was too easy, so I rarely went to class: I would show up, answer the roll call, and then go to work. The travel agency mainly handled Japan, Thailand and Malaysia visas, plus delivering and collecting passports at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. I was paid not per item but NT$14,000 a month, and the working hours were very flexible as long as the job got done. Back then we worked on Saturdays too!</p><p>My daily work plan went roughly like this: at 8:30 in the morning I went into the office to see what needed delivering that day. If I had a class at 10:10, after picking up the documents I would ride from the company on Minsheng East Road straight to the Interchange Association, Japan on Qingdao East Road to submit them, then ride back to Xinzhuang for class and roll call, calculating all the way which stops to make after noon. Sometimes I would also collect documents from clients in Xinzhuang, Banqiao and Sanchong on the way.</p><p>What documents did I collect? Mostly passport application documents: photos, application forms, original ID cards, and sometimes cash for tour fees. When I got to school there might be several hundred thousand NT dollars in cash and several important personal documents in my school bag. After class I would ride my Kymco Haomai scooter out of the school in a hurry, heading from Xinzhuang to Taipei Bridge to handle another batch of customers' visas. The Thai and Malaysian visa documents had been sorted by the OP (operations) ladies at the company in the morning and put together in a kraft paper envelope. Visa applications were handled at the head offices of Thai Airways and Malaysia Airlines at the corner of Dunhua North Road and Minsheng East Road, which were often packed and bustling in the afternoons. Then I'd ride back to the Interchange Association to collect the Japan visas that had been processed, and go to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to pick up newly issued passports. Thinking about it now, it all seems like such fun. I would often pick up a customer's brand-new passport and smell the new document, and I envied the people who could go abroad to play in those days. I imagined this little green passport leaving this small island to travel the big wide earth. Perhaps that's why I stayed in this travel industry for so long.</p><p>Now, looking back, I realise this job had many risks. Although I had labour insurance, I am glad that I rode carefully thirty-some years ago. Helmets were not required at the time, but I had bought a full-face Zeus helmet, and on Taipei Bridge the fastest I ever rode was only 100. On top of that, I was paid only NT$14,000 a month, but my bag always held a hundred-odd thousand in NT dollars, US dollars and banknotes of various countries, to be delivered back to customers in batches, and at every stop I had to count and check carefully.</p><p>Between 1992 and 1993 I worked as a document courier for two years and rode through every street and alley of Greater Taipei. I have been on almost every road. There was no Google back then, so you had to keep a street map in your head, and a sense of direction mattered all the more, otherwise after a few turns through alleys you wouldn't know which way you were facing. There were no mobile phones to keep in touch either. I just had a pager hanging from my waist, and when the company needed something it would page me, and I would call back from the customer's place to confirm. I'm also grateful that no money ever went missing. Many thanks to the OP ladies for their care and for sorting the documents. I only had to follow the planned route and finish the to-do items one by one.</p><p>Today I read this story about a delivery rider and it struck a chord! Everyone is working hard just to get by. So when I see my vocational students go to part-time jobs after class, put on full-face helmets and post a story, my heart gives a jolt. I hope they come and go safely and smoothly, like the ladies at the travel agency who looked after me back then, who always told me: "College boy, ride carefully!"</p>
