<p>My old employer, China Credit Information Service, publishes its latest survey report on Taiwan's 5,000 largest enterprises every year. Do you know which five insurance companies in Taiwan had the highest revenue in 2022, after the pandemic? How will they develop in future?</p><figure class="ph">Photo</figure><p>Among the large enterprises ranked within Taiwan's top 50 by net revenue (<a target="_blank" rel="noopener" href="https://www.credit.com.tw/CreditOnline/sample/2023Top5000_06.pdf">2022 China Credit Information Service report</a>), there are as many as seven life insurers, so you can imagine how large life insurance's share of Taiwan's overall economy is. The ranking is shown in the table above, with Cathay, Fubon and Nan Shan ranking as the top three; by number of employees, Cathay and Fubon each have 20,000 to 30,000 staff. Revenue in 2022 was a winter for the life insurance industry because of the pandemic, and with such a low base, the growth rate and financial reports of Cathay and Fubon in 2023 can be looked forward to.</p><p>In the last century, I was an assistant researcher in the marketing department of China Credit Information Service, responsible each year for writing the ranking report, while also using this valuable market data to reorganise and help various companies with market analysis, or to help write listing proposals. Companies I handled include Sakura Construction, Chingyun Electronics and Zuanquan Enterprise; those I helped with capital increases include Taifeng, Tungtex and Changsheng Enterprise (then the operator of the airport MRT); Nomura Securities at the time also commissioned China Credit to write an industry impact report on Taiwan's accession to the GATT (now the WTO). I spent at least 3 months at the World Trade Center library working day and night to finish the Chinese version of this report, which Nomura Research Institute and the Interchange Association, Japan then translated into Japanese and published in Japan for industry reference.</p><p>While working at China Credit, I lived in a rented place in Sanchong, and every day rode with tens of thousands of other scooter commuters down Taipei Bridge to work in Taipei's Songshan District, arriving at 9 and leaving at 9. There was no two-day weekend then, so I worked at least 6 days a week, which is just the same as the "996" work system now popular in China; it was really a realistic experience of Taiwan's fresh graduates "selling their livers". With low pay and long hours, I do not remember how much I earned! And I left no memories either. The most real thing is that in those two years I began to lose my hair, slowly losing the full head of hair of my youth, which was not worth what I gave up.</p>
