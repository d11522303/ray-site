Admissions Expo

The one who went to see the department expo at the NTU Azalea Festival was me. The Little Puppy and his classmates went to explore departments they are interested in. Which school or department they will study in the future is still undecided, and we all respect and support that.

University admissions today are like a travel fair: lively and varied. I spent nearly two hours in the NTU gymnasium, and I was very envious that high school students from all over could see the campus and environment before enrolling. The courses are all published online, and the professional expertise and teaching methods of the department professors are presented as much as possible, letting students with interests in various fields think carefully about their learning direction.

After two hours of walking, and asking quite a few questions in order to get small souvenirs, I have a few small thoughts.

#ElectricalEngineeringAndMedicineHadTheBiggestCrowds

On a Sunday afternoon, I only saw professors present at the Electrical Engineering booth explaining the department's features. Other booths, some of which posted times when teachers would explain, mostly had students as the main presenters. Perhaps because of this, the Electrical Engineering crowd was huge. I think to enter the prize draw you had to answer questions about equations that I completely couldn't understand (the EE students kept stressing that they were very basic), but checking in also worked.

For Medicine and Dentistry, because of the second-stage written exam and practical lab exam, I overheard many students asking seniors about the practical exam (cram schools already offer related courses: ten mock lessons for NT$88,000). There were long queues and one-on-one questions, and they looked truly serious about understanding it well.

#SecondStagePreparation

I asked students from several departments how the second-stage oral exam is arranged. Quite a few departments said there is no oral exam, only a written one. The College of Engineering sets a uniform exam in mathematics, physics and chemistry, deeper and harder than the General Scholastic Ability Test. All students who pass the first stage at three times the quota take one more exam, ranked directly by their subject scores.

The business school places great weight on the oral exam content. Take the Information Management department as an example: it will arrange three oral exam rooms with three groups of teachers. The first stage is an interview with an English self-introduction to see speaking skills and bearing; the second is reading aloud an English academic paper, after which the professors ask you about it in English and you must answer in English; the third is a few maths questions.

#LearningPortfolio

High school students today are pitiable, having to make learning portfolios, and we are all the victims. So I also asked the university students there whether admission had any special requirements for the portfolio content, and most of them could not answer whether it really matters.

For popular departments such as electrical engineering, computer science, medicine and dentistry, students enter the first stage by their General Scholastic Ability Test scores. At the second stage, separate exams and practical tests carry an important share, and unless the portfolio content is really striking enough to catch the professors' attention, grades are still what matter most.

Overall, I think that because future study in NTU's College of Science and Engineering still requires high-intensity maths and science ability, it is understandable to test related abilities to decide admission. But the humanities, social sciences, business, biology, earth sciences and so on place great weight on students' interests and aspirations, so the share of the learning portfolio should be valued in application admissions. Professors should be able to tell at a glance entries that are too "watery" or ghost-written, and reject them directly.

#CourseArrangement

Since I want to study for a doctorate, I went to look at several departments that I could apply to and was interested in, to chat with students. At one department's booth there was hardly a visitor and only the student was left minding the booth; at another, the students were very enthusiastic and even displayed many teachers' textbooks and materials on site.

I asked: Do you know what the situation of senior doctoral students in your department is?

He happily said: In one course, the seniors come to teach and lead discussions, the whole semester is just the seniors.

I asked back: Then what about the professors?

The student happily said: The professors drop in now and then to see how they are learning.

Me: Wow, thank you. Then may I take your little gift?

Student: Of course.

Me :)

#Summary

I suggest that parents and students who want to study a department must go and see that school and department, chat with the seniors to see whether they themselves like what they study, and also ask the teachers and see whether you can find the teachers at all. Never let the ranking systems and rankings of cram schools decide your future, truly.
