[My First Travel Agency Job: Episode 1]

In 1992, the summer before my second year of university, I took my first job with labour insurance: Daya Travel Agency. From the point of view of today's vocational students, it would count as a summer internship worth 3 credits, but back then I was only working to earn money, and the job didn't stop when summer ended. I didn't take my studies at Fu Jen very seriously, and since the work was mainly riding around delivering documents, I could always earn a bit extra.

#Travel Agency No. 005

It was an old travel agency in one of the many twelve-storey apartment buildings around the junction of Minsheng East Road and Zhongshan North Road. Boss Lo said he had taken it over, and that it was the fifth licensed travel agency in Taiwan. Walk in the door and there was a sofa reception area, and at a glance four or five desks. Apart from Boss Lo, who had a small partitioned office, only the accountant had a room of her own; the other colleagues sat at desks facing each other, coordinating departures to various places. Smoking was allowed in the office, and Boss Lo and a few colleagues smoked, so I remember the office always full of smoke and a bad smell. Add a few dim, dust-caked fluorescent lights overhead, and the whole company felt yellowed and out of date, like an old negative.

#Planning the Collection and Delivery Route

I was the company's new errand boy, and the only university student at Agency No. 005. My work was mostly collecting and delivering documents. First thing in the morning I'd find out where to collect from, mainly the documents customers needed for visas. Before noon I'd bring all the customer documents and fees back to the office and hand them to the OP lady, then rest and get ready to deliver the sorted documents to the visa offices in the afternoon. The afternoon routine was fairly fixed. The biggest batch of visa documents went first to the Interchange Association, Japan, on Jinan Road, perhaps collecting a few more customer items on the way. Then I'd ride to Thai Airways on Dunhua North Road for Thai visas, and to the Malaysian visa office, whose location I can't quite recall now. By then my shoulder bag was much lighter. Back at the office I could chat and plan the next day's route. The work was easy and I was on the road most of the time. In an age without mobile phones or pagers, it was a carefree job.

​#Reading Odd and Even Street Numbers

Anyone who has done courier work in the Taipei area will know the rule "southeast even, northwest odd". Cities around the world put odd and even numbers on opposite sides of the street, so buildings on one side all go up in odd or even numbers. That let us errand boys judge roughly how far a destination was from the number, without straining to read house numbers while also watching out for Taipei's terrifying traffic.

Take my alma mater, Fu Jen Catholic University, at No. 510 Zhongzheng Road, Xinzhuang City, Taipei County. The campus gate is on Provincial Highway 1, the north-south trunk road, so by its number the university sits on the east side of Highway 1, and so on.

#My Least Favourite Route

Today many of Taipei's main roads have bus lanes and the MRT, and the traffic and cityscape are much tidier. What I disliked most back then was delivering along Nanjing East Road, sections 1 to 5. From Yi-Die, Yong-Chi and Sincere department stores to Daya Department Store, almost all our partner companies were along Nanjing East Road, and buses, trucks, cars and scooters stopping at the roadside fought for lanes and parking spots with no order at all. My poor, gallant little White 125 scooter saw me through nearly two years of courier life, through the streets and alleys of Taipei City and County.

#Would I Do It Again?

Looking back, I used my own scooter for company deliveries, rushing all over Taipei for a little over ten thousand dollars a month, with the danger and fatigue of the traffic besides. University was hard to get into in those days, yet once in, I didn't spend the time to settle my career direction, earn certificates, study my specialty, or prepare for civil service or graduate school exams. Instead I spent my young brainpower and energy on a meagre wage, which wasn't worth it at all. From the point of view of social mobility, this was class fixity: I thought I was safe and ordinary at the time, but I was actually at risk without knowing it.

Because of that ignorance, a few unusual things happened on my deliveries. Thinking about them now, if the me of those days did the same today, I might really end up in the news.
