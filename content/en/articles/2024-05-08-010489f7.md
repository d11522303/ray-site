---
title: "Only When the Tide Goes Out Do You See Who Isn't Wearing Pants"
excerpt: "Childhood summers at my grandparents' home in Qiding, Miaoli: playing at the beach without pants, simple meals, and memories sparked by a Mukoda Kuniko book."
published: true
---
<p>Memory is like a frayed skein of wool: once you find the end, you can pull and pull without stopping. (From Mukoda Kuniko's "Father's Letter of Apology" - "The Student's Ice Cream")</p><p>Only when the tide goes out do you see who isn't wearing pants.</p><p>As a child I often went to the beach to play, and to keep my clothes from getting wet, I was the kid with no pants on. I took them off myself and left them on the shore, then wrapped a big black rubber inner-tube ring around my little bottom so that no one could see I wasn't wearing pants. Every summer, a few days before returning to Taichung, I would sometimes go to my maternal grandparents' home in Qiding, Miaoli, and wait there for my paternal grandfather to come from Taichung to pick me up. Some years I spent the whole summer in Qiding. Calling it my "maternal grandmother's home" feels a bit odd; I prefer to say my maternal grandparents' home. Perhaps in my memory this household was full of people: there was the blind great-uncle who always sat in front of the door, several whetstones beside the doorway, where my grandfather and uncles often sat on stools sharpening the kitchen knives they worked with, including large cleavers, long fish-gutting knives and delicate little knives for carving vegetables and fruit. There was also my grandmother, who often sat sighing in her room, and what I heard most from her was that she felt dizzy and unwell. At the back were two wood-burning stoves, where family members were often to be seen. This poor family had given up my fourth aunt for adoption, my mother and my third aunt had both married young, and the aunt closest to me in age was my playmate, so what I saw instead was my uncle lighting the fire and cooking.</p><p>The dishes that came to the table were nothing fancy. Most often there was fried small fish caught from the sea, stir-fried sweet potato leaves growing everywhere in the garden, radish stir-fried with eggs laid by the hens and ducks in the front yard, and sweet potato rice with so much sweet potato that you could barely see the rice. Once cooked, it was kept in an aluminium pot, and when we were hungry we lifted the lid and ate, mostly cold, and I have no memory of everyone sitting down and eating together. Only when I grew up did I realise how hard-won the happiness of having the whole family sit down together for a meal really is.</p><p>Because I often played in the sea and the creek as a child, my playmates were all kids about my age. At that time Qiding beach in Miaoli had a public-run bathing beach that charged admission, but once inside there was no supervision at all. I remember black rubber inner tubes being left out for visitors to take freely. Why would we locals pay to soak in the sea? The coastal windbreak forest was our adventure land and playground anyway, and from any gap in the casuarina trees you could walk out to the ocean. But seawater is sticky, so after coming ashore you always had to find fresh water to wash and put on clean clothes before going home. Being a child was best: you could follow the adults into the shower area with a big water trough in the middle without anyone noticing, and after rinsing off with fresh water you could strut out through the front gate and walk back to my grandparents' house.</p><p>In my memory, Qiding bathing beach was vast, its iron-grey sand lapped by waves of not-so-clear water. The murky seawater meant that we kids weren't likely to be noticed without our pants, though with nothing on below the waist it was a bit chilly and never very pleasant, so I mostly kept a little pair of underpants on for a sense of security. Forty or fifty years ago, the bathing beach was packed with people during summer vacation. Taiwanese people had few ways to relax and enjoy themselves back then, and the cheap or free seaside was always a good choice for family fun, though as I recall I never went with my parents. It was just a few wild kids at the beach who called each other along to play in the water, look for shells and build sandcastles, until the sun set and, hungry, we walked home along the path.</p><p>What was special was that as we walked along the road, we paid special attention to a wild vegetable much like A-choy, growing in clumps along the roadside. The kids would pull and pull as we went until our hands were full, then at home chop it up with a hatchet to feed the chickens and ducks. It was the duck greens (yazai cai) that my grandmother's ducks loved.</p><p>Mukoda Kuniko's "Father's Letter of Apology" is a collection of memoirs, linking the small things and observations of family life with strings of words. The Japanese translation sits very close to a native tongue, and I recommend borrowing it to read. You'll probably feel, as I did, a slow, tranquil sense of remembrance. If you recall something from your childhood, or if, like me, you once played in the water with a bare bottom, give a smile or a heart to let me know.</p>
