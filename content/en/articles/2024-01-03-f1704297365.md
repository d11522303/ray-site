Times Change and Things Move On

As the new year begins, the course for the Vietnamese international class is also nearing its end. Over a year of classes and interaction, I have slowly gotten to know all 41 students from all over Vietnam. Since I took part in a short student exchange visit to Nong Lam University in Ho Chi Minh City in 1995, my classmates back then are probably the parents of this group of students now, so it feels very familiar that their children come to study in Taiwan and meet them in my classroom.

The photo taken at Nong Lam University in Ho Chi Minh City shows my Vietnamese classmates of those days, and now I have Vietnamese students in my class. Before the trip abroad, the chair of the Fu Jen sociology department, whom I called Boss, instructed me over and over not to be too flashy by shopping and showing off wealth, because 30 years ago the standard of living in Vietnam was some way behind Taiwan's. Today, Vietnamese students find Taiwanese tuition reasonable, and living in rented rooms in New Taipei with the transport is acceptable to them. At a meeting today I also heard that many private vocational high schools have brought in large numbers of foreign students to make up for the shortage of local ones, with foreign students reaching nearly half of enrolment, becoming the bread and butter of private-school teachers.

The Vietnamese class at HWU is the spring intake. I am not very clear about the channel through which they came to study in Taiwan, but it must be through the university's International Affairs Office recruiting on the ground. The students told me that the first semester's tuition is subsidised by the Taiwanese government and that they pay for the following seven semesters themselves. I was curious and asked what university tuition costs in Vietnam. They said it is about the same as in Taiwan, 40,000 to 50,000 NT dollars a semester, so studying in Taiwan is an economical and good-value choice.

For a full four years, these students are like Taiwanese university students: they take part in campus courses and activities during class time and, after class, go to work part-time just as their Taiwanese classmates do. On holidays they can travel freely around Taiwan, with no one managing them as a group and no collective work in factories. On their Instagram or Facebook you can see records of their trips and food all over Taiwan, and I am quite envious. The students can't all be called diligent, and their phones never leave their hands. Their whole outfits are bought online, and they don't lack for trendy shoes and clothes. I hear they have their own channels, which are much cheaper than Taiwanese retailers.

Now attendance in class every week is very high, and in recent weeks everyone has been present with no absences, handing in assignments and sitting exams without exception. When I hand back marked handouts, students receive them with both hands, make eye contact and say thank you, which is very polite.

Becoming their teacher is a matter of fate. A few days ago in class I told the students I would like to learn Vietnamese, and asked how much they would charge for an hour of tutoring.
