Mountain Movers

Because I was moved by watching Beyond the Lonely Railway in the South, and because I have listened many times to Director Hsiao's podcast interviews, I have always had a fondness for documentaries, since there is always something real in them, and even a one-sided account is still a point of view.

We bought tickets and watched it at the Ambassador Theatre in Ximending. Actually, two months ago there was a free campus premiere, but the screening time fell at the busiest moment for our family, so we decided to spend New Taiwan dollars to support good Taiwanese film production instead. Below are the three of us and our most subjective impressions.

Me:

First, although some people say the film just finds old people to tell the story, I think that if we do not leave images and words now, later the story will be explained by whatever media or writing comes along, which is not at all objective, so I strongly agree with and support this approach. I admire even more that some key figures in the film did not appear on record. I think the director must have invited them many times, but everyone has their own convictions, and that too is a way of speaking, isn't it?

Second, using the key position of the semiconductor industry to explain Taiwan's situation today is one angle, and one accepted by most people. But I do not accept this single narrative of survivors or winners. Taiwan's difficulties and position at this moment do leave those of us living on this island feeling helpless, but there are hard workers in every trade. At least I think that the efforts of Taiwan's official tourism bodies over many years to gain exposure around the world are also a kind of soft diplomacy, letting outsiders know us and letting Taiwanese living abroad identify a bit more with Taiwan, and that matters very much for the survival of this island.

The university student:

He thinks the seniors who went to RCA to learn semiconductor design, process and management were amazing, especially how in a very short time they found every way to learn (COPY) all the material by copying it here and there. In that era there were no mobile phones, only paper to record things. He admires that way of setting aside personal pride and dignity just to achieve what the country expected.

He says that the IC design he learns now is all written in software, and that even though first-years only study 1980s-level courses, they are already so busy they are going crazy. He thinks that projecting etched IC circuits was truly impressive, and that the RCA team seniors were gods. He also learned for the first time this time why his school's department buildings carry the names of well-known companies: industry and academic knowledge are completely integrated in the Hsinchu area, with no gap between study and practice.

The university student's mom:

The one who first had the urge to see the film is here. Without her insistence, I think we could have waited for it to come on MOD and watched it several times. Mom has her own impressions and I cannot express them for her, so I only record a few of the points she mentioned:

1. Learning needs role models and leadership. Taiwan's semiconductors sprouted from the RCA plant, but what mattered was that there were exemplary leaders guiding it. Only forward-looking people with wisdom and ability could gather talent, carry out the goals, and decide the direction and steps so that learning became results.

2. Whatever you do requires persistence and continuity. These smart and wise senior engineers were mostly top scorers in the joint entrance exams back then, and they all had a persistence and perseverance in their profession, which is what gave them a chance to succeed.

3. Raising points for discussion is also an important kind of sharing. Each of us in the family had a different view of the film. If each had watched it separately on a TV, a phone or a screen, it would only have been individual impressions and could not have become a family memory...

Indeed, after watching Mountain Movers, our family went to eat at Mei-Guan-Yuan, and recently we have been asking the university student to drive. Today he specially navigated us into the Emei parking lot, of intermediate difficulty. He thought this car park in Ximending was fun. Although he often came to this area to eat and shop in high school, both of those things, eating here and going to the movies, are things his parents did when we were at university. This time we invited the university student to experience it together, creating a memory different from before, and that is also good.
