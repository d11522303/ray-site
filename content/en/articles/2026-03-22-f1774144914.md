▋Boss Lo and His Friends: The Travel Agency Delivery Boy

In the summer of 2025 I bought a brand-new Yamaha JOG scooter and registered an account on Foodpanda to join the online testing and sign-up, originally hoping to do part-time delivery work in the summer holiday. I was a delivery rider to begin with, but what I delivered was not food but all kinds of visa documents for a travel agency.

Thirty-odd years ago, anyone who wanted to travel abroad had to prepare lots of documents. Besides the passport, whether to nearby countries such as the Philippines, Malaysia, Thailand, Japan and Korea, or farther away to the US, Canada and Europe, every country needed a visa. So for a trip through several European countries, the visa fees alone were a considerable expense. But that was exactly what kept us document runners in business.

In 1992-93 (ROC years 81-82) I was in a university day program. Because the content of my department was too easy, I seldom went to class. After attendance was taken I would go off to work. The agency mainly handled Japan, Thailand and Malaysia visas, plus submitting and collecting passports at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Pay was not per piece but NT$14,000 per month, with very flexible daily hours as long as the work got done. And in those days Saturday was a working day!

My daily work plan went roughly like this: at 8:30 in the morning I went into the company to see what needed to be delivered that day. If I had a class at 10:10, I would take the documents and ride directly from the company on Minsheng East Road to the Interchange Association, Japan, on Qingdao East Road to submit them, then ride back to Xinzhuang for class and roll call, calculating all the way which stops I had to run to after noon. Sometimes I also collected documents from customers in Xinzhuang, Banqiao and Sanchong along the way.

What did I collect? Most of all it was passport application documents: photos, application forms, original ID cards, and tour fees in cash, and so on. Sometimes when I got to school my bag held several hundred thousand NT dollars and several important personal documents. After class I would ride off in a hurry on my Kymco Hao Mai, from Xinzhuang toward Taipei Bridge to handle another batch of customers' visas. The Thailand and Malaysia visa documents were arranged in the morning by the OP sisters at the company and put together in a kraft paper envelope. The visa offices were at the head offices of Thai Airways and Malaysia Airlines at the corner of Dunhua North Road and Minsheng East Road, often packed with people in the afternoon and very lively. Then I rode back to the Japan Interchange Association to pick up the completed Japan visas, and to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to collect new passports. Even now it seems so much fun. I often held a customer's new passport and sniffed the smell of a new document, envying the people who could go abroad to play in those days, imagining this small green passport leaving this little island to travel the big wide earth. Perhaps that's why I stayed in the travel industry so long.

Looking back now, I realise this travel agency job carried many risks. Although I had labour insurance, the insurance system was still very imperfect more than thirty years ago. Luckily I rode carefully. Wearing a helmet was not required then, but I had bought a full-face helmet, and even on Taipei Bridge my top speed was only 100. On top of that I earned only NT$14,000 a month, yet my bag always held a few hundred thousand, in NT dollars, US dollars and banknotes of various countries, to be delivered back to customers in batches, and at every stop I had to count and confirm carefully. Writing this, even my scalp tingles.

Between 1992 and 1993 I worked for two years as a document courier, riding through the streets and alleys of Greater Taipei. I have been on almost every road. In those days there was no Google Maps, so you had to keep the street map in your head, and a sense of direction was important, otherwise a few turns through alleys would leave you lost. There were no mobile phones either. I just had a pager at my waist, and when the company needed me they would page me, and I would call back from the customer's place. I am also fortunate that no money ever went missing. I really thank the OP sisters for their care and for sorting the documents. All I had to do was follow the planned route and complete the tasks one by one.

Today I read this story about a delivery rider and it resonated deeply! Everyone is working hard to survive, so when I see my students working part-time after class, putting on full-face helmets and posting stories, my heart skips. I silently hope they go out and come back safely and smoothly, just like the travel agency sisters who looked after me back then. They always said to me before I left: "College student, ride carefully!"
