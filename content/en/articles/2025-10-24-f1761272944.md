[How to Comfort Disappointed Students]

Yesterday the results of the second round for a well-known airline's internship that our students had applied for arrived. The department had put forward 11 students, with all the résumés still handwritten, and only 2 students got through to the interview stage. When the notification email came, besides being surprised, I thought for a moment about whether to notify students individually or make an announcement.

In the end I decided to announce it directly, so all the students would see who made the list!

As you would expect, the internship preparation group went silent. It wasn't that nobody responded; sadness, frustration, disappointment and other feelings filled the virtual space.

We teachers were in the group too, and our feelings were also mixed. Their homeroom teacher even came to find me, hoping to say a few words and encourage everyone. At that moment I was troubled as well, wondering whether I should say something.

It was noon, right after four classes. Their homeroom teacher had just finished four periods of volunteer teaching at a senior high school and had just driven back to campus, and would go on to four more periods of on-campus classes in the afternoon. Respect.

I gulped down my rice. What I was really swallowing were my own feelings and disappointment, and the food filling my stomach was to get myself pumped up first, so I would have the spirit and strength to face this group of students who had worked hard for a good while.

I decided to sort myself out first. After eating, I prepared four job-hunting preparation sheets, including a list of study certificates, a full-year internship intention form for parents and the student, and a personal information form, and I prepared a sample for writing a 104 résumé. With food in my stomach and some thoughts in my heart, I walked into a classroom full of low pressure.

STEP 1. I carried a big pile of empty document folders, each with an individual student's name on it, showing that I had come prepared for every single student.

STEP 2. I printed the forms above one by one on A4 paper and laid them out at the front of the classroom. Students whose names were called took their folders and counted the items one by one in their own hands, using action to sort out their feelings.

STEP 3. I didn't say a word about this morning's announced results. I first told all the students how to use the order on the folder to prepare their materials: those with nice handwriting could fill in the forms by hand, and those with poor handwriting could type them in character by character. In writing, with order and words, they would come to understand themselves and think about their future direction.

STEP 4. I gave support. If students had any questions, their homeroom teacher, I, or any teacher in the department could help, but when they came to ask, they had to have complete materials in hand and not come empty-handed.

STEP 5. I told the students who were accepted to prepare their interviews and English interaction skills as soon as possible and to give the interview everything they had. If there was the slightest sign of giving up or an offhand attitude, the interviewers at a well-known company could see it at a glance.

STEP 6. I set a deadline: after the holiday, students must hand in all the materials in full, in order, inside their folders, collectively to the class representative, who would organise them and give them to the teacher. I also asked everyone to pay attention at all times to the department website, email and announcements in the group, and to give feedback, building good habits for the community.

How do you comfort disappointed students?

Fortunately my students are all adults, though mostly 18 to 22 years old. At this point I personally feel that the teacher's role is to support and to practise alongside them. Every licensing exam, competition preparation, interview material preparation and internship job is an important learning opportunity for these young people, and also a good time to understand the realities of society.

And I gain even more myself. It lets me look back at whether I am bravely facing the things I have been avoiding, because the two characters for "teacher as role model" (師範) are a verb, not a noun.
