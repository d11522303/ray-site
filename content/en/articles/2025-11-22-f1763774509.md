【An Inclusive Classroom】

I've been busy scheduling courses lately. For a teacher, each semester's course arrangement completely shapes the next half year's schedule, research and writing time, and links to recruitment activities, so this is the most important moment.

In recent years, in the first semester, the General Education Center has often put a required general education course, World Tourism Geography, into my timetable. The students come from every department in the university and are assigned by the system rather than choosing it themselves, so I get to know many students outside the Department of Travel Management, with around eighty students in every class.

This year's course is special, and the classroom is not the specialised room in the tourism college that I'm used to, but a large flat ground-floor classroom in the College of Design. Although it is packed with connected university-style desks and chairs, it can seat more than eighty students. I like this seating arrangement best, because students always have somewhere to write when they take notes and fill in worksheets, and of course it's also harder for them to drag seats around on their own to form little cliques in class.

Perhaps because it is a flat ground-floor classroom, the school has assigned quite a few students with mobility difficulties to the class. Around 8:10 in the morning they arrive with breakfast and bags big and small, sitting in the front seats by the door. They also leave a larger space for the classmate who is least mobile and uses a wheelchair. They help each other, greet each other, pass each other worksheets and so on, and it is lively and cheerful from early morning.

In fact I rarely chat with students in class. From the start of the lesson to the end, it is all lecture. But this week was a bit different, because after midterm week there is less pressure to cover material, so I took the initiative to ask the students in the front row about their learning. Of course the students didn't say anything careless, but when they later talked to me about the long-term discomfort and its effect on their studies from cerebral palsy, surgeries, poor body coordination and so on, it really made me take it to heart.

The topic began with a wheelchair that is always left at the front of the classroom. Three years ago I bought one just like it for my grandmother. Because its design is more ergonomic, the person sitting in it feels more supported and will not shift left, right, forward or back due to a slight build or lack of strength, which is more considerate for someone who sits for a long time. Another student, who has an easier time getting around, also shared how the difference in the length of his legs causes him to fall often, and because he is slight he always bumps against bone and ends up with more injuries.

A ten-minute break is too short to chat much, and mostly they spoke and I listened. At that moment, as a teacher, I felt that what I had been complaining about in my heart that morning (not enough sleep from getting up early, having too many classes and losing my voice, standing too long and getting sore feet) and the little dramas that sometimes play out in my mind were simply a sin. In a class of over eighty, the students who are learning and thinking are still the majority. Staying in the classroom and doing their own part is a duty, and also a privilege of time and health. People without such conditions cannot even walk into this classroom.
