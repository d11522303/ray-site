---
title: "So This Is What It Means When Someone Buys Insurance for You"
excerpt: "Policyholder, insured and beneficiary are different roles. Learn why the policyholder holds the real power and how to check your own insurance contracts."
published: true
---

<p>Have you ever heard a conversation like this?</p><p>"Mom bought you a life insurance policy. In twenty years you can get back NT$750,000 at maturity, and you have lifelong medical protection too. And if you pass away there is NT$1 million for the beneficiary!"; "From now on you need to prepare NT$5,000 a month for the premium. Think of it as savings. You'll thank Mom later!"</p><p>Not quite understanding, having never signed an insurance policy, you sign one out of your family's [good intentions]. Twenty years later you go back and ask your family: the premiums are all paid, can I get the money now? By then you may be just buying a house, raising a newborn or paying your children's tuition, only to find that the money was collected by your family, and nobody ever told you. When you go home to ask, you only get a casual response.</p><p>"Oh yes, Mom took that money first. Once I'm old you won't need to support me."</p><p>You are speechless, and since it is your closest family, it is hard to explain. If your spouse finds out, they may be so angry they stop speaking to you.</p><p>If you are in this situation, it is actually because you are not familiar with the contents of the policy and got confused by the several key parties on it.</p><p><b>Policyholder</b>: Mom<br/><b>Insured</b>: Son<br/><b>Beneficiary</b>: Daughter-in-law<br/><b>Payment account</b>: Son's account</p><p>An insurance contract is a financial product, and because it intertwines relationships between people, you must be very clear about the four key parties above.</p><p>1. The insured is listed first in the contract, meaning the policy exists because of this person; but the <b>most powerful person in the whole contract</b> is the <b>policyholder</b>.</p><p>2. It seems natural to pay the monthly premium for yourself, but the <b>maturity annuity is collected by the policyholder</b> and the <b>death benefit is collected by the beneficiary</b>. The insured amount can only be used when the insured has an accident or falls ill, so it looks like you are the sucker.</p><p>3. And the <b>policyholder has the right to change the beneficiary</b>; the insured son only has the right to consent, so one day when the mother-in-law is unhappy, she can change the beneficiary back to herself at any time.</p><p>To avoid family discord and to keep you from losing both money and goodwill, I suggest you go back and review your own insurance contracts. First, for every policy you are paying for, the policyholder should be yourself. If it isn't, arrange a day to go together to the insurance company and change it in person. Second, if you are paying and the insured is not you, or the beneficiary is not who you thought, have a good talk together and likewise go there in person to make the change.</p><p>Let me repeat: in an insurance contract the <b>policyholder</b> is the most important. If you are the one paying, go online right away and check the content of your policy.</p>
