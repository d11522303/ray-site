Cogito, ergo sum! René Descartes

I think, therefore I am! Descartes

I recently read an interesting popular-science philosophy book, *The Truth at the Edge of the World* (by Guangzi), which discusses the relationship between the self and the world. Starting from whether light is a wave or a particle, the uncertainty principle in quantum mechanics broke my basic belief that the world is completely scientific and rational, and changed my thinking about whether what we see and experience is real.

Interestingly, at almost the same time, my little puppy, studying at school, heard a professor mention related ideas in class. I quote the reflections from the assignment below so the two can be compared, but first let me share my own observations.

The book says that human thought and consciousness come from the brain, and the active brain is trapped in a completely closed space. Without a neural network connecting to the outside, the brain lives only in a pitch-dark, sealed environment. How, then, does the brain understand the world? How does it control what it perceives, assembles and thinks? Yet the brain's neurons communicate, discuss and interweave with one another to form consciousness. But can that be what we regard as fact? Or is it the illusion of a dream?

In "The Adjustment of Controversies" (Qiwulun), Zhuangzi dreamed that he was a lifelike butterfly, and on waking he could not tell whether he was Zhou who had dreamed of a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming of being Zhou. Zhuangzi questioned the boundary between true and false, and between self and non-self, as if there were no absolute distinction between what is seen in a dream and what is seen awake.

-------------------------

Consciousness is the sum of the neural electrical activity among about 140 billion neurons in the brain. The brain sits in the completely closed cranial cavity, pitch black inside, and brain cells "construct" a vivid three-dimensional world from neural electrical signals arriving from outside. It is like a person who has been shut in a dark room since birth, from outside which come telegraph beeps of "di-di-du-du". From the pitch and order of those sounds he infers the scene outside, but he has never directly "seen" what is outside. Outside, it may well be all probability waves, yet he imagines an image of particle states (Guangzi, 2021).

-------------------------

This is a very interesting question. When people are ill or unwell they take medicine, using drugs to change the brain's cognition so that they "feel" better and to change the body's perception and responses. The uncertainty principle in physics and Zhuangzi's butterfly dream arise from physics and philosophy respectively, but both point to the vagueness and indeterminacy of the nature of reality and cognition. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states that microscopic particles cannot be measured precisely at the same time. Both discuss the unmeasurability and un-assertability of absolute judgement, exploring deeper perspectives in mechanics and philosophy, and there is an interesting resonance between them.

So what people see is not necessarily real. What is real, then? Or is it illusion? What is in the brain and what is in the actual environment are mirror images. But I believe that the whole world is real and the geographical environment truly exists, while the existence of life is rather a reflection of consciousness. Different souls or modes of thinking lead to different thoughts and views about the world. Just as with the same event, because each individual sees it differently, one unconsciously experiences it completely differently, down to the smallest differences in taste and mood. Everyone's view of the world cannot be fixed. Whether the world is good or bad is absolutely tied to one's own experience and expectations of life, and in the world of cognition, the world is what is real.

So the author of the book summarises three expectations for life:

1. You are important and the world revolves around you. You are not a passer-by in this world but the leader of your own worldview.

2. Think independently. Don't yield to anyone's pressure, and keep the steering wheel of your life in your own hands.

3. Face the world with sunshine and love. At the core of human nature lie seeds of sunshine and love, which you need to nourish so that they grow and sprout.

#ContemporaryCognitiveNeuroscience_BrainAndMind

Class reflections, 17 December 2025

This session on "consciousness" covered many questions I often think about on my own, including abstract ones such as "why am I me". The first thing I want to record is that the teacher mentioned: consciousness is a concept constructed by ourselves. This concept divides consciousness into the classical theory and the predictive machine theory. The classical theory matches intuition and the science we have learned in the past: we take in information from the outside through perception and sensation, and the brain analyses it and produces cognition. The predictive machine theory, however, holds that the brain presupposes that the world has a set appearance (constructed from previous experience and memory), and uses sensory information to confirm whether its predicted cognition is correct. Yet the process of confirmation is still influenced by the brain's presuppositions, so perhaps the world is absolutely subjective under each person's perception. In explaining the predictive machine theory, the teacher mentioned "Bayes' theorem" as support, but did not go into the theory in detail, so I looked up additional material and add here what I understood of it. Put simply, for everything we start with a preconceived judgement, and after seeing new information we adjust our view; the rule for adjusting how much we originally believed is "Bayes' theorem".

From a mathematical angle, Bayes' theorem is conditional probability, with the formula (posterior) = (prior) * (likelihood) / (evidence), all calculated with probabilities. For a simple example, suppose a disease test has a true positive rate and a true negative rate (likelihood) of 99% each, and the real incidence of the disease (prior) is 1%. Then the (evidence) is 99% * 1% + 1% * 99%, and our degree of belief becomes (99% * 1%) / (99% * 1% + 1% * 99%) = 0.5. That is, because 99% of people were not sick to begin with, this affects our perception and judgement of a true positive, lowering our trust in the test to only 50%.

Another cognitive point that impressed me was when the teacher explained that, looking at inner speech from the brain's perspective, different brain regions are actually used. After understanding the neural mechanism of inner speech, I began to look again at a feeling I have had for a long time: when I am just "thinking about things", thought is often continuous and natural, but once I clearly realise that "I am talking to myself", my original train of thought is interrupted, as if I must pause thinking to observe what I am doing. In the past I thought this was just a lack of concentration, but from the perspective of brain science, it is actually the result of the brain switching between different cognitive modes.

According to the handout the teacher provided, spontaneous inner speech mainly involves brain regions related to language processing and semantic connection, while when a person becomes aware that they are having an inner dialogue, brain regions related to self-monitoring and metacognition are additionally engaged. These two states are not completely unable to coexist, but out of considerations of energy and efficiency the brain tends to activate them in turn rather than running both at full power. This also explains why, in situations of high self-awareness or anxiety, thinking becomes stuck, because frequent self-monitoring interferes with the flow of thought itself. This understanding made me realise that when I need to think with focus, moderately reducing my monitoring of my own thinking process actually makes my thoughts clearer and smoother.
