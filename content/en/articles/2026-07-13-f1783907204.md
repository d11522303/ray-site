#UCLA

Applying to go to the US as an exchange student was something already written into my child's learning portfolio for university admission. We believed that once the learning goal was set, over the following four years of university they would find the time and look for opportunities to try the challenge. After enrolling, a roommate had won an American university exchange place last year, and the other two roommates (including my child) felt that this seemed like a good chance to broaden their horizons during their studies. With the mindset of emulating those who are better, they pushed ahead through the on-campus application stages of document review, interviews and applying to the partner university departments, and the long university summer vacation opened up another direction and journey.

The funding opportunity came from a scholarship from my child's department. Each year it offers 10 places to first-year students and 10 to second-year students to apply for an 8-week summer programme at the electrical engineering department of a partner overseas university. Classes can run for half a year or in the summer term. Once the award is announced, you can apply for enrolment at the university. Taking the University of California system as an example, you have to pay registration fees, tuition per credit and dormitory fees. For tuition, Berkeley charges USD 600 per credit and UCLA USD 460, and I heard Stanford is even more expensive. UCLA also offered on-campus housing and dining hall options, and given worries about safety in the US, among several universities my child chose to go on exchange to UCLA Electrical Engineering, based on last year's classmate's experience and the scholarship's funding cap.

At the same time, such opportunities are not rare at other universities in Taiwan. Top universities of course have them, but there are many competitors. In fact the private technological university where I work has them too, such as the MOE "Dreams Come True" overseas internship programme that I recently won together with four students, and the recent Youth Billion-Dollar Overseas Dream Fund programme. It is a pity that when I promoted these opportunities and challenges to students, among the nearly one hundred students in the four classes where I spoke, not many were willing to try it together with their teacher. This is education's dilemma. The reason behind students' indifference may be that they have to work part-time to earn money! Maybe they think English is hard! Or maybe they find this kind of thing boring, making work for yourself, when lying flat is so much more comfortable?

Back to the discussion of costs. The Ministry of Education's "Dreams Come True" programme, like the Youth Billion-Dollar Overseas Dream Fund that so many people have been sharing on Threads lately, only requires that students have their own ideas and plans. They can discuss and brainstorm with teachers, plan the project well within the deadline, write it up as a concrete proposal, and then submit it online or by mail to try their chances against the other nominated units. If you apply, you have a chance; if you don't, you have nothing. With the selfless sharing of experienced teachers, students' dreams become reality, and with strong support from the Ministry of Education and the school, they fly ten thousand miles away.

In fact the scholarship funding is not high, and cannot compare with the upkeep and depreciation costs of the luxury cars that students at private school campuses drive. But that is a choice, and Taiwan's social values are diverse. Anyone who keeps an attitude of actively striving and a view of life of working hard to develop is worth encouraging. In particular, if a student drives a luxury car to school because they opened their own shop and earned the money through their own business, I, a low-paid private school teacher, feel equally proud, because that attitude of striving upward and moving forward is the same.
