[University Admissions Results Are Out: How Do We Read the Numbers?]

This is the second cohort under the 108 curriculum. Judging from each school's admission growth rate published for 2022 to 2023 (ROC years 111 to 112), almost all of them declined. Only National Taiwan University, National Cheng Kung University, National Tsing Hua University, National Chiao Tung University, Taipei Medical University and a few specialised schools grew. For schools with an admission rate below sixty percent, where there are too few candidates and many applicants apply repeatedly, even moving quotas over to the subject-test allocation will not make things much better. But once all admissions are finished, it is the schools in the middle of the ranking that lose the most students, while schools in the lower half do not necessarily recruit worse than the middle ones.

Why? Because the current admissions system is the result of coordination. My reasoning is as follows:

1. Top universities keep their traditional advantage and are getting harder to get into, with registration rates close to 100%.

2. Mid-ranked universities, public or private, that offer programmes similar to those at the top universities mostly receive waitlisted candidates from the recommendation and screening route. These are students who respect the system and its design, but because of the falling birth rate there are fewer students overall, and the further down the ranking order a school sits, the fewer students it can take, so registration rates fall between 50% and 80%.

3. Lower-ranked schools, under the current system, can only take a few students who don't understand the situation (for example the 4 students at the now-closed Mingdao University). Whether through individual application or the subject-test allocation, hardly any new students remain, so I infer that their registration rates would all be below fifty percent.

But the registration rates announced in October turn out rather different! Because of a special arrangement called [independent admissions], some schools that fell short of their quotas can choose to run their own admissions. They can attract the last group of students who don't want to prepare application materials and don't want to take exams, and just want a university diploma, by offering no exam, no portfolio review, and an easy path to graduation. As a result, their final registration rates also land between 50% and 80%.

So the registration rates published by the Ministry of Education are a lagging indicator. If anything, the individual application stage may be where we can see students' real choices and thinking.
