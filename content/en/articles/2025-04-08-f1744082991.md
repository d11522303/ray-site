Trump's tariffs shake economies worldwide: what should Taiwan's tourism industry do?

Although Trump's tariff war has hugely affected Taiwan's overall exports and economic mood, people always love to travel and will simply shift to reasonable and suitable destinations, not retreat across the board as they did during the pandemic.

In particular, Taiwan, as a short-haul travel hub in Asia, still has the potential to welcome regional travellers whose bookings are diverted. If neighbouring markets such as China, Japan and Southeast Asia continue to prefer short trips, Taiwan can win more of the Asian regional tourism dividend by strengthening tourism cooperation with East and Southeast Asian countries, easing visas and adding flights.

But strengthening the substance of Taiwan's travel offerings is even more important. Abroad, we can no longer make cheapness or friendliness, which are low-value-added points, our marketing focus. We should emphasise local cultural experiences and sustainable tourism, and encourage national policy to focus on investment in and incentives for domestic tourism hardware and software, as well as supporting plans such as the people-centred transport and sustainable eco-tourism that international tourists value.

Encouraging local governments and central agencies at all levels to increase internal investment in the tourism service industry and to focus on marketing perspectives, in order to improve the current situation, and stabilising the expectations of industry workers and consumers for economic and political stability, is the key to all Taiwanese jointly facing major international uncertainties.

The following is excerpted and adapted from a report by Wit reporter Yeoh Siew Hoon, 04/04/2025

https://www.webintravel.com/trump-tariffs-and-travel-how-will-the-turbulence-play-out-in-asia/

Former US President Trump announced yesterday historically high tariffs on many countries, hitting global trade hard and putting Asia's tourism industry on high alert. According to the BBC, this wave of tariffs has been described as "a truly astonishing move" that could lead to "the collapse of the business models of thousands of companies, factories and even whole countries."

Except for Singapore, which was charged only the 10% baseline rate, other Asian countries such as Taiwan and Indonesia (32%), Thailand (36%), Vietnam (46%), and Cambodia and China (49%, 54%) all face heavy tax burdens, seriously affecting regional economies and the tourism industry.

Chris Hemmeter, Managing Director of Thayer Ventures, believes global interdependence cannot be ignored. Although uncertainty is up in the short term, in the long run tourism remains resilient and able to recover. He added that Asia-Pacific (APAC) may remain relatively stable in this turmoil, because Chinese travellers may travel less to the US and choose regional travel instead.

https://www.thayerventures.com/

In Singapore, Nicholas Cocks, a partner at the travel-focused investment fund Velocity Ventures, worries that if Southeast Asian countries cannot retaliate with tariffs, they may instead retaliate with visa restrictions or entry taxes, further hurting tourism. And if market uncertainty continues to spread, it could lead to a global recession, which would then cut corporate and personal travel budgets.

https://www.velocityventures.vc/

However, intra-regional travel may become a "safe haven" for Asian tourism. According to Marriott International, the share of its guests within Asia has risen from about 38% before the pandemic to nearly 60%. PATA and ForwardKeys point out that as of 2023, 80% of Asian travellers' international trips went to destinations within the region, up from 70%-75% before the pandemic.

In addition, the World Bank estimates that by 2030 the Southeast Asian middle class will grow from 135 million to 334 million, which together with the rapid recovery of low-cost airlines brings strong momentum to regional short-haul travel. OAG data for 2024 show that short-haul flight capacity has recovered to 95%-100% of pre-pandemic levels, while long-haul clearly lags behind.

https://www.oag.com/

Chinese outbound travel also shows a clear inward shift. Since the end of 2023, over 60% of Chinese outbound tourism has chosen Asian destinations, reflecting realistic considerations of economic pressure and uncertainty.

Hannah Pearson, director of the Malaysian tourism research firm Pear Anderson, looked back at the 2008-2009 global financial crisis and noted that Asian tourism, relying on its own markets, was relatively less affected at the time. She predicts that even if high tariffs affect major source markets such as China, South Korea and Japan, Asian travellers will still turn to short- and medium-haul trips.

https://www.pearanderson.com/

The article is optimistic: Asian travellers may no longer fly so far or spend so much, but they will still travel.
