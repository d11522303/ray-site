[International Students: 1+4 International Program] EP3

The attached photos are notes from our first-year Vietnamese students in the Foundations of Tourism class. Think back to your own primary school days when you were first learning Chinese characters, and how you tried every way to fit all the strokes correctly inside the squares. Then look at these students, who have studied Chinese for only a few months, handing in worksheets like this every class. I often stare at the students' notes in a daze. In this age of smartphones and digital life, how precious it is to have the ability to learn and the drive to improve.

Next, let me explain what the [1+4 International Program] is. In short, you come to Taiwan for 5 years of study: the first year is for learning Mandarin, and the following 4 years are in a professional department. Outstanding students have the chance to stay and work in Taiwan. This year the HWU Department of Travel Management has a class of students who have started, now in a year of Mandarin training. We look forward to their listening, speaking, reading and writing of Chinese becoming better once they enter the formal system.

If I were an outstanding foreign student under financial pressure and very interested in Taiwan's semiconductor design and manufacturing, could I apply? Of course there is a chance.

In 2025, a total of 63 colleges and universities in Taiwan applied for the [1+4 International Program], formally named the "Plan to Expand Recruitment of Overseas Chinese, Hong Kong and Macao Students, and Foreign Students in Key Industry Fields." Its main recruiting departments are in Taiwan's six key industries: e-commerce (including information processing), service fields, manufacturing, construction, agriculture and long-term care. Off-campus internship courses can also be arranged, so it is legally feasible for students to study and work at the same time.

Which schools take part? The attached image lists a website where you can look up how to apply. Because most are special classes recruited by individual schools, students may not study in the same class as Taiwanese students, and this premise may need to be explained to foreign students first.

All of the above schools should be recruiting independently. The day before yesterday I heard of a fellow teacher who is spending three days and two nights in Vietnam to run oral interviews at 5 schools, which is very hard work. The Taiwan government has not arranged any joint entrance exam or similar; it depends on how many faculty and facilities each school can offer in its special application to the Ministry of Education. To learn more about individual schools, you have to ask the responsible office on each school's website. At HWU, our International Affairs Office and Language Center handle the project application and student management, while the courses are designed and arranged by each department.

Looking at the notes of students in class and thinking of how they practise speaking and recognising Chinese in class, I feel that I, as a teacher, should actually go and study a foreign language, to better understand these students from afar and their beautiful homelands.
