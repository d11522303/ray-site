[How Many Years of Schooling Is Enough?]

In my own case, from compulsory education through to my master's degree, I spent exactly 19 years in school in my life, and that does not even include kindergarten. If kindergarten were added, it would truly be a very long time. Yet how I got through it is very hazy, and when I try to recall what happened at school, I can remember very little.

[Most people in Taiwan hope for a university degree or higher]

The landgeist map shows expected years of schooling, with the Four Asian Tigers occupying four of the top five places. Taiwan's average on the map is 16.5 years, shown in purple for 16 and above, meaning people expect to hold a university degree or higher, just like the citizens of South Korea, Hong Kong and Singapore. In fact, in 2019 the average number of years of schooling for Taiwanese aged 25 and over was 12.3, so most people hold a high school diploma. Among Asian countries, the people of Pakistan (8.7/5.2), Yemen (9.1/3.2) and Syria (9.2/5.1) have expected years of schooling below ten, meaning that these countries only hope their children can get through junior high school. Set against the actual years of schooling, the educational opportunities of these people are a chance that is almost impossible to come by in life, which truly breaks my heart.

[Dreams are grand, reality is harsh]

Turkey, which recently changed its name, is the one special country that appears on the maps of both Europe and Asia, and it is also the country with the biggest gap between expectation and reality in the education indicators. Turkey's expected years of schooling are the highest in Asia at 18.3, and Turkish parents hope their children will earn a master's degree, but in fact its average years of schooling rank near the bottom among European countries, only 8.1 years, which is junior high school graduation. This is expectations that are too high while the support provided by society and the state is not enough. Is it the people's problem? I think it is a problem of national governance and social culture.

[Studying matters, but other factors must come with it]

The top places in the UN's Human Development Index (HDI) are all European countries, so learning from advanced countries must be the right direction. On the map of average years of schooling in Europe in 2019, you can see that most European countries leave school life after finishing high school and enter the workplace and professional fields. Taking age 25 as the basis, blue means 13 and above, and the top three are Germany, Switzerland and the UK. This is an integrated picture: the health and longevity and quality of life of European countries should be the key indicators in which they win out. Of course, learning more knowledge advances human development, but if quality of life and health do not keep up, it is all in vain.

The data for the maps above come from the UNDP's Human Development Index (HDI). As shown in the chart below, the HDI comes from three dimensions: a long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living; the knowledge dimension is further divided into two sub-indicators, expected years of schooling and mean years of schooling. I have attached the link so you can click in and find the original data. I could not find Taiwan included in any ranking in the UNDP's public information, so how the landgeist map site obtained its data for Taiwan remains to be verified.

All in all, this is the moment when university application results have just been released, and the July subject exams are still torturing our hard-working students. Is studying really that important? As a teacher, I subjectively agree that education is very important, and that getting into a good school is certainly right. But if, after entering school, we forget to acquire knowledge and expertise, if we do not properly build our personal health and habits, and if we have no sense of belonging to or care for the community, then even the longest period of schooling will not necessarily move society upward.

I would like to ask everyone: in the future, how many years of schooling will your child go through, from small to grown?
