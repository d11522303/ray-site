【Daughter of the Great Harbour】

Elections are coming, and all of Taiwanese society is heating up again. I have supported every colour with my vote at one time or another, sometimes right and sometimes wrong, and that's fine. This is real democracy.

I can decide whom I want to support myself, and if I vote wrongly I can vote again next time. If I say something wrong, I won't be purged or have to flee for the rest of my life, and a teacher can talk about all sorts of things in class without suddenly disappearing.

-------What follows are my reading notes----------

I took this book off the library shelf because of its author, Chen Rou-Jin. Years ago I read her "The President's Relatives" and felt deeply that words can describe so many relationships. Set against my own time at China Credit Information Service, when I co-compiled the "Top 500 Taiwan Enterprises Ranking" with senior colleagues, it was as if I could see through the relationships between people in society all over again.

Sadly the author died suddenly in a traffic accident in 2021, and this book should be the representative work in which she wrote Taiwan stories with her pen and delicate female perspective. The story starts from Kaohsiung Harbour, which I know very little about, and uses the backdrop of Taiwan's modern history of being colonised and ruled. It follows the lifelong effort, gentle persistence and hard work of a successful woman entrepreneur, Ai-Hsueh, in her upward journey to the present day, to inspire every Taiwanese person born on this island who is resourceful, flexible and perseveringly tenacious. It portrays every Taiwanese person striving to sort out a future within a diverse culture, and describes a road that belongs to Taiwanese people.

In my cruise classes I often tell students that big ships are usually named with female references, so you seldom hear of a cruise ship called Alexander or William; they are named Princess, Queen, and words of beauty and fragrance. A ship is like a woman who carries everyone on board, embracing and protecting them as she brings everyone and everything safely toward where their hearts long to go. In the author's words I also deeply felt that the Taiwan island, the daughter, the wife and the mother she describes all carry everyone and everything they embrace toward their destination, as steady and solid as a great ship.

In recent years the international community has not been optimistic about Taiwan's situation. If you read this book you will find that for Taiwanese people, turbulence and unease are the norm, and persistence and optimism are the soul of Taiwanese people. We cannot ask the big neighbouring power to stop talking about us all day, but we can build for ourselves a persona that belongs to Taiwan. We are the crew of a huge mother ship in the Pacific. Where the island is, we are; where we are, home is.
