#TheStoryOfWashingDishes

Since I was little, I have had one special skill: I am very good at washing dishes.

I could take a mountain of bowls and plates stacked higher than the sink and sort it, layer by layer, into categories. With a sensible amount of water and detergent, working efficiently, from large to small and from many to few, I would rinse the great pile of dirty dishes clean of grease and leftover soup, find the different kinds of plates and pots in the suds, and place them one by one in the draining area, then arrange them neatly in the dish dryer, press the 30-minute hot-air button and close the door. After wiping my hands dry, I could pour a glass of warm water and sit at the dining table waiting for the clean, white, warm dishes to come out.

From the lower years of primary school, I could finish the job of washing the mountain of dishes left by a whole table of eating and drinking in a dozen or so minutes, and all of this was because our family often had guests over to drink and eat. Actually, for a child, among all the drinking snacks on the table full of drinkers, the bowls and plates I really used were perhaps one to three items at most. I did not drink alcohol or any other drinks, and the things a child can eat are only a few. When everyone had drunk and eaten their fill and the guests switched to sitting in front of the television with tea to sober up, my mother had already been exhausted from cooking and preparing so many dishes. The last job, clearing up, naturally fell to the eldest child in the family, to take care of the whole table.

I never thought this was unfair. Besides being used to it, I felt I was good at it, and all my life I have still felt that my parents loved me, because sharing household chores is something everyone who shares the same space should do.

What is more, when I left hospital after a serious illness in 2003, I came across an article by Dr. Chuang Shu-chi saying that sick people in recovery should cook at home, and that after eating they should treat washing dishes as rehabilitation: skilfully lifting and putting down the bowls and plates, with warm water running over the hands in a kind of massage, which brings great benefits to health. Being so practised at washing dishes, I loved all the more to report to the sink on my own after the family finished a meal.

This should also count as a kind of practice of closeness in the family, at least that is how I have felt about it.

I wonder whether a good counselling psychologist would have another layer of interpretation, as interesting as the one from the person in the comments.
