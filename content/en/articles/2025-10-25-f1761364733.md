[The Beauty Industry]

Over the years there have always been students who come to our university of technology from vocational high school beauty programmes with cooperative education. When we chat, I ask whether their hands are all right, and whether they still itch or hurt. They often show me their beautifully manicured hands and say: Teacher, since the cooperative education placement ended and I stopped washing hair, it's been much better. But beneath their young make-up I see knuckles thicker than those of ordinary girls, and it pains me.

When I worked at the Y17 Taipei Youth Recreation Center, we often hosted staff training and morale-raising meetings for hair salon groups. Many different groups came, always for annual or monthly meetings, and our venue rental team loved them, because they were generous, unlike many organisations that haggle for discounts and bring up whom they know to save a little on the venue fee. When such a group holds an event, it is just like direct selling and insurance companies: bonuses and honours are handed out, everyone wears a sash, and the emotional value is delivered in full, so that every worker on the front line feels a sense of achievement and keeps going for a living. Every trade produces its champion.

Years ago, when I was taking travel agency manager training at the New Taipei Labor Conference Center, the woman sitting next to me was the boss's wife of some "Little Lin", though I wasn't sure whether it was Little Lin Eyeglasses, Little Lin Hair Salon or Little Lin Pancakes. She came to class every day looking very elegant. Since we were classmates, we chatted a little. I asked why she was taking the training, and she said her company often organised trips and always hired outside travel agencies, so she wanted to get trained and open her own to make that money herself.

In the past, Taiwanese people often said that so-and-so's child was "doing hair", or that if you didn't study you would go and be a "hair washer", and so Mandom, Little Lin and others are everywhere. It's nothing but the Taiwanese spirit of hard work. Bosses, for the sake of money, find every way to add storefronts, cut costs, and increase membership numbers and prepaid fees. The labour behind it came from an endless supply of cooperative-education students in the past, with strikingly low internship allowances of a few thousand dollars a month, at the cost of their health and their young years.

This article on Taiwanese-style hair washing is very well written. I hope that one day a student of mine will have the skills, the capital and the management ability to take Taiwanese-style service into the world the way Yun Chi Fang and Din Tai Fung have, charging high service fees, so that the "beauty industry" becomes another Taiwanese point of pride.
